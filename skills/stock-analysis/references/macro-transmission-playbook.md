# Macro Transmission Playbook

Use this reference to determine which macro variables matter to a specific company and how to express their effects without substituting narrative for evidence.

## Start from company exposure

Map the business before selecting macro variables:

- revenue by product, customer type, and geography;
- recurring versus transactional demand;
- discretionary versus essential spending;
- pricing power and contract duration;
- labor, commodity, logistics, and energy cost exposure;
- debt maturity, interest-rate mix, and refinancing needs;
- reporting currency and foreign revenue or costs;
- regulation, subsidies, tariffs, and government procurement; and
- valuation duration, especially how much value depends on distant cash flows.

Ignore macro variables that lack a material transmission channel. A complete list is not a better analysis.

## Test the main channels

### Growth and demand

Connect GDP, employment, industrial production, consumer spending, housing, or capital expenditure to a specific end market. Identify whether the company is early-cycle, late-cycle, defensive, or dependent on a narrow spending category. Confirm the channel with orders, bookings, traffic, units, utilization, backlog, or another company KPI.

### Inflation, pricing, and costs

Separate selling-price inflation from input-cost inflation. Test timing: costs may reset immediately while customer contracts reset later. Use gross margin, unit cost, pricing, mix, and inventory data as confirmation. Do not assume inflation is positive merely because nominal revenue rises.

### Interest rates and credit

Separate three mechanisms:

1. customer affordability and demand;
2. company interest expense, refinancing, and liquidity; and
3. the discount rate applied to equity valuation.

Do not claim that lower rates benefit every stock equally. Highly profitable cash-generative companies and externally funded long-duration companies have different sensitivities.

### Currency

Distinguish translation, transaction, and competitive effects. A stronger reporting currency can reduce translated revenue while lowering imported costs. Use disclosed geographic revenue, hedging policy, and constant-currency results when available.

### Commodities and supply chains

Identify whether the company is a producer, consumer, transporter, or price-taker. Trace commodity moves through volume, realized price, input cost, inventory, working capital, and customer demand. Account for hedges and contractual pass-through lags.

### Policy, regulation, and geopolitics

Separate enacted policy from proposals and political statements. Identify the legal or operational milestone required before financial effects begin. Map the exposure to market access, compliance cost, subsidies, taxes, tariffs, licensing, or supply continuity.

## Build the transmission statement

Use this form for each material macro driver:

> **Observed variable and date** -> **industry mechanism** -> **company exposure** -> **affected KPI or financial line** -> **next confirmation signal** -> **invalidation condition**

Example structure, without inserting unsupported values:

> If credit conditions remain restrictive, financing-dependent customer demand may weaken; for the company this should first appear in orders and backlog, then revenue growth. A recovery in orders despite unchanged credit conditions would weaken this macro explanation.

## Avoid double counting

Do not count closely related observations as independent evidence. Policy rates, bond yields, valuation multiples, and growth-stock price action may all reflect the same rate shock. Likewise, commodity prices and producer margins may represent one transmission chain rather than two confirmations.

## Set confidence

- **High**: the macro fact is current and primary-sourced, the company exposure is disclosed, and an operating KPI confirms the mechanism.
- **Medium**: the macro fact and exposure are clear, but company confirmation is incomplete or lagged.
- **Low**: the exposure is inferred, the source is weak, the data is stale, or several mechanisms could explain the same result.

Confidence measures evidence quality, not outcome probability.
