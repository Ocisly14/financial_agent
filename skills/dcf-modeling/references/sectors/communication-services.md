# Sector playbook — Communication Services (GICS Communication Services)

Covers three GICS industries with almost nothing in common: Diversified Telecommunication Services and Wireless (T, VZ, TMUS, LUMN), Media & Entertainment (DIS, WBD, NFLX, FOX, PARA, LYV), and Interactive Media & Services (GOOGL, META, PINS, MTCH). What ties them together is also what breaks a single chain for all three: each is a story about which asset a company reinvests in to keep its claim on the customer — spectrum and network plant for carriers, a content library for media, and now data-center infrastructure for platforms — and that asset routinely sits outside where a generic capex line expects it. Decide which of the three models an issuer belongs to, or which model each segment belongs to for a diversified issuer, before writing a single forecast row.

## Chain shape — what to build instead of the default

### Wireless/telecom carriers

Build revenue from subscribers × ARPU per line of business (postpaid phone, prepaid, broadband/fixed wireless, business wireline), with churn and net adds as the mechanism — never one blended growth rate; a stable-looking blended number can hide a shrinking postpaid base offset by a promotional prepaid push. Forecast wireline as its own declining stream converging toward a small residual, not faded to zero growth as if it stabilizes. Build a combined network-investment row (capex plus spectrum) for reinvestment — see Reinvestment.

### Media & entertainment

Decompose consolidated revenue by type (affiliate/carriage, advertising, direct-to-consumer subscription, theatrical/licensing) before forecasting anything, because the consolidated line nets a declining annuity against a growth bet and tells you about neither. Content spend is the reinvestment; content amortization inside cost of revenue is the maintenance charge — a capex-versus-D&A parity check run on reported capex is answering the wrong question here. Where a legacy segment funds a streaming build-out, carry the two segments' economics, and eventually their terminal fates, separately.

### Interactive media / ad platforms

Decompose revenue as volume (impressions, ad load, or engaged users) × price (per impression, per ad); a platform can grow by raising load, engagement, or price, and each has a different ceiling. The AI/data-center capex surge has moved the largest platforms from asset-light to capital-intensive inside a few years — model it as a named, dated infrastructure program layered on the historical base, not noise the reinvestment ratio fades back through. Keep any non-advertising line (subscription, cloud, hardware) as its own stream.

## Revenue — the decomposition that pays

### Wireless/telecom

Forecast gross adds, churn, and ARPU separately per line of business — a rising ARPU can mask an eroding subscriber base or vice versa. Postpaid phone net adds are the primary growth engine and the most promotion-sensitive; prepaid carries lower ARPU and higher churn and tracks the value segment; broadband/fixed wireless access is the newest growth vector and belongs on penetration of a stated addressable footprint, not a flat rate; wireline is a structural decliner. Equipment revenue and equipment-installment-plan (EIP) receivables move together and are financing, not organic growth — see Working capital.

### Media & entertainment

Split into affiliate/carriage (subscriber count × contracted rate, count declining), advertising (rate card × audience, cyclical), DTC subscription (subscribers × ARPU net of churn and price increases), and theatrical/licensing (episodic — forecast per title or as a normalized multi-year average, never smoothed into a growth rate). Track streaming ad-tier revenue as its own line once material; it carries platform-style economics closer to Interactive Media than to the linear ad line it often gets folded into. A mid-single-digit consolidated growth number can be a double-digit-declining affiliate base netted against double-digit DTC growth — the net tells you about neither.

### Interactive media / ad platforms

Where a platform discloses users and revenue-per-user separately, forecast both: revenue-per-user captures monetization intensity independent of audience growth, and the two persist for different reasons. Treat any non-advertising line (subscription, cloud/enterprise, hardware) as its own stream with its own economics rather than folding it into a blended "platform growth" number. Advertising itself correlates with GDP and marketing budgets more tightly than most streams in this sector — expect it to be the most cyclical revenue line you model anywhere in the business.

## Cost and margin — the level the story lives at

### Wireless/telecom

Margin lives at the line-of-business level, not consolidated — wireline's structurally lower and falling margin drags against wireless's stable-to-improving one, and a single EBITDA-margin assumption hides that offset. Device subsidy runs through cost of service or cost of equipment depending on presentation; check which, because a promotional cycle shows up as margin compression in one place and revenue softness in the other, and reading only one misdiagnoses the cause. Network-sharing and cost-synergy programs are named, dated initiatives with a start and a run-rate — not a smooth margin trend.

### Media & entertainment

Content amortization is the dominant cost-of-revenue line and the true expression of the reinvestment story: rising content amortization funding a growing DTC base is a different bet than the same ratio funding a shrinking one, and margin read without subscriber growth alongside it misdiagnoses which is happening. Sports rights are fixed, multi-year, contractually escalating obligations independent of any year's viewership — model the disclosed rights-fee schedule, not a ratio to revenue that flexes with audience. A margin forecast improving purely from marketing discipline while content spend keeps rising is a different story than both falling together.

### Interactive media / ad platforms

Separate traffic-acquisition-cost or revenue-share payments to distribution partners from platform-owned infrastructure cost — the two carry different margin trajectories as AI-driven depreciation flows into cost of revenue. Headcount cost (R&D, sales and marketing) has been this sector's flexible lever; treat a disclosed efficiency program as a named event with a run-rate, not a smooth drift. Rising depreciation from the AI build is now showing up inside cost of revenue and opex, not just on the balance sheet — a margin forecast that ignores it treats a real, disclosed cost as still off-model.

## Reinvestment — capex, D&A, and what maintenance means here

### Wireless/telecom

Network capex is permanent and high and does not fade toward a lower mature-industry level; each technology generation (4G to 5G, now fiber densification and fixed-wireless) resets the reinvestment need rather than letting it decay. Spectrum purchases are the sector's structural peculiarity: they are usually capitalized as an intangible acquired in periodic FCC auctions and often reported outside the capex line entirely — a parity check run on reported capex alone looks clean while missing the largest periodic cash outflow the business makes. Build a combined network-investment row (capex plus spectrum/intangible network-asset purchases) for any reinvestment or FCF-quality read, and compare it to total D&A including spectrum amortization, not reported capex to reported D&A.

### Media & entertainment

Cash content spend is the real capex; content amortization is the real depreciation — run any reinvestment-intensity read on that pair, never on reported capex (studio real estate, streaming infrastructure) against reported D&A, which answers a much smaller question here. Keep physical capex as its own small row so it is not inflated by conflating it with content investment. The content library carries no reliable book value once amortized — the forecast question is future content spend and its conversion into subscribers and viewing, not the historical asset balance.

### Interactive media / ad platforms

Historically asset-light; the AI infrastructure build has taken the largest platforms into capex ratios several multiples of their pre-buildout level inside a few years. Model it as a named, dated program with its own trajectory and an eventual plateau tied to a disclosed capacity target — do not fade back toward the pre-program ratio, and do not extrapolate the current build rate to infinity either. Depreciation lags capex sharply during a ramp this steep, so a capex-to-D&A gap during the build years is timing, not a defect to close by cutting the capex forecast. Read the issuer's infrastructure-financing disclosure (owned versus leased versus off-balance-sheet structures) before assuming all of it sits in the capex line.

## Working capital — the cycle and its sign

### Wireless/telecom

Equipment-installment-plan receivables are a major, growing item distinct from ordinary trade receivables — a rising EIP balance funds device sales the way a captive finance arm would, and ignoring it misses a real, growing use of cash even while reported margins look stable. A mix shift toward prepaid changes the working-capital ratio independent of any change in the underlying business, since billing-cycle timing differs from postpaid.

### Media & entertainment

Content-related payables (participations and residuals, contracted rights payments not yet due) are a structurally large liability that grows with content spend — carry them in the operating working-capital base even though they sit outside the conventional receivable/inventory/payable triad. DTC subscription collects cash before or as service is delivered, pushing that piece of working capital negative, while the content-obligation side pushes the opposite way — net the two rather than importing a legacy-media sign for the whole company.

### Interactive media / ad platforms

Working capital is typically a small, mild use or source of cash: advertiser receivables net against payables to publishers and traffic-acquisition-cost partners. Do not import a heavier, positive working-capital assumption from a physical-goods sector. Where a platform runs marketplace or payments functionality, funds held for others can inflate the balance sheet without being an economic use of the company's own capital — exclude them from the operating build.

## Tax and WACC notes

Effective tax rates diverge sharply across the three and do not converge to one sector number. Carriers pay close to the statutory US rate — domestic-heavy income, with large deferred-tax balances from accelerated network depreciation shifting timing rather than the effective rate itself. Media runs close to statutory as well, given home-market-heavy production and revenue. Platforms carry the lowest effective rates of the three because a material share of IP and earnings is domiciled outside the US — treat that historical gap as a level under pressure from global minimum-tax phase-in, not a stable anchor, absent direct evidence it persists unchanged.

Beta spans the widest range of any GICS sector. Carriers sit at the defensive end — regulated-adjacent, high-dividend, low revenue cyclicality — despite carrying the sector's highest leverage. Media sits in the middle, tracking the advertising cycle. Platforms sit at or above market, driven by ad-cycle sensitivity and, more recently, by capex-cycle and AI-narrative sensitivity. Never apply one "communication services beta" to an issuer spanning sub-models.

Carrier leverage is a deliberate, management-targeted capital structure, not a residual of the business, and dividends are load-bearing for the equity story — a WACC or capital-structure assumption treating carrier leverage as transitional misreads the industry. Media and platform capital structures carry no comparable structural leverage target; read each issuer's own stated policy rather than assuming a telecom-style target applies.

## Terminal — what steady state means here

### Wireless/telecom

The terminal question is genuinely "does this become a utility": in steady state, subscriber growth converges to population/household growth, ARPU growth converges to inflation-like pricing, and the durable variable is margin and capex discipline rather than growth. A terminal growth rate materially above GDP is a claim that new-line-of-business economics (fixed wireless, private networks) or consolidation keep compounding indefinitely — say so if you assume it. Spectrum reinvestment does not disappear at maturity; terminal reinvestment intensity must still carry a normalized, amortized allowance for periodic spectrum renewal alongside physical network capex, or terminal free cash flow overstates what the carrier can actually distribute.

### Media & entertainment

Terminal value for a business whose declining segment funds its growing one is this sector's hardest case: decide explicitly whether the linear/affiliate segment has run off to a small residual by the terminal year (terminal economics essentially the DTC business alone) or persists at a lower steady level, and do not carry today's blended margin forward as if the mix stops shifting at the horizon. Once DTC scales, content spend as a share of revenue should settle at a level consistent with a mature subscription business defending its base and raising price, not the elevated ratio typical of a subscriber land-grab.

### Interactive media / ad platforms

The open terminal question is whether the steady state looks like the pre-AI asset-light business or whether AI infrastructure becomes a permanent, elevated cost of staying competitive — closer to a capital-intensive utility than a software platform. Pick one explicitly; fading back to the pre-buildout capex ratio is itself a claim, not a default, and needs the same evidence a claim of permanence would. Regulatory and antitrust exposure (breakup, forced divestiture, ad-targeting restriction, mandated interoperability) belongs in the terminal case as a named scenario or a sensitivity on terminal margin and growth, not as a near-term line item.

## Terminal-state calibration bands

The table below describes the steady state only — the economics each business settles into once growth has normalized and the current cycle, build programme, or subscriber land-grab is over. It says nothing about next year and must not set a near-term assumption: the explicit years are anchored on the issuer's own history and causal case, and a sector band sitting next to that evidence would compete with it and win for the wrong reasons. Terminal is the one place the issuer's history cannot speak, because it is by construction the state after everything the history covers.

Read the table as triage, not as a target. An assumption inside its band is ordinary and still needs the ordinary rationale; one outside it is a claim that this issuer's steady state differs from its sector's, and that claim needs evidence naming what makes it durable.

Two constraints bind every row below. Terminal capex and D&A converge, because steady state replaces the asset base rather than expanding it — each reinvestment band is the one level both approach, on the combined asset definition stated for that sub-model. And terminal growth cannot exceed long-run nominal GDP.

## Failure modes specific to this sector

- **A telecom parity check run on reported capex alone.** Spectrum purchases routinely sit outside the capex line as an intangible-asset purchase; a capex-versus-D&A check that omits them looks clean while missing the largest periodic cash outflow the business makes.
- **Wireline faded to zero growth instead of forecast down.** It is a structural decliner; treating its growth rate as merely "slowing to flat" overstates consolidated revenue every year it stays in the model.
- **A media capex-versus-D&A check run on physical assets.** The real reinvestment is cash content spend against content amortization; the physical-asset pair is immaterial and answers the wrong question entirely.
- **A consolidated media growth rate netting a declining annuity against a growth bet.** The blended number can look steady while affiliate revenue collapses and DTC revenue does the offsetting work — forecasting the net obscures which lever the story actually depends on.
- **Sports rights modeled as a ratio to revenue.** They are fixed, escalating, multi-year obligations independent of any year's viewership; a ratio-based forecast lets a bad ratings year silently shrink a cost that is contractually fixed.
- **AI capex faded back to the pre-buildout ratio during an active, disclosed build program.** The buildout is a named, dated event with its own trajectory; reverting to the historical asset-light average ignores the program the issuer has already committed capital to.
- **One sector beta applied across sub-models.** A defensive telecom beta and an above-market platform beta describe different businesses; using either for the other issuer moves WACC in the wrong direction.
- **EIP receivables left out of telecom working capital.** They fund device sales the way a captive finance arm would; excluding them from operating working capital hides a real and growing use of cash behind stable-looking reported margins.
