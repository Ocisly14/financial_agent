# Sector playbook — Real Estate Investment Trusts

Covers equity REITs across property types — industrial and logistics, data centres, towers, retail and malls, residential, office, healthcare, self-storage, lodging and other specialty. The structural fact that breaks the generic chain: the engine computes free cash flow from an income statement whose largest expense is depreciation, and discounts reported earnings and book value as if they were real. For a portfolio of well-maintained property, book depreciation is not an economic cost — the assets often hold value or appreciate over the schedule the accounts assume — so reported net income and reported book value are close to meaningless anchors here. Real estate services firms (brokerage, property and investment management fee businesses) carry ordinary capex, working capital and GAAP earnings and do not belong on this chain at all; route them through the generic sector treatment instead.

## Chain shape — what to build instead of the default

- Discard net income and book value as anchors. Build three measures explicitly: net operating income at the property level (property revenue less property operating expenses, before general and administrative cost, depreciation and interest) as the true earnings measure; funds from operations (net income plus real estate depreciation and amortization, less gains on sale of depreciable property, adjusted for the issuer's share of joint-venture and non-controlling results) as the cash measure the sector actually reports; and adjusted funds from operations (funds from operations less recurring maintenance capital, tenant improvements and leasing commissions, less the non-cash straight-line rent adjustment) as the measure that funds the distribution.
- The gap between funds from operations and its adjusted form is real, non-optional reinvestment that a naive chain omits entirely by treating book depreciation as if it were the capital the business consumes. Do not let a capex row set to the depreciation schedule stand in for it.
- Build a net asset value cross-check: forward net operating income capitalized at a market rate, less net debt and preferred claims, plus the issuer's share of unconsolidated joint ventures at their own implied value. This is the discipline that keeps a discounted-cash-flow answer honest, because unlike most operating businesses the underlying assets trade in a real market with observable prices — a discounted-cash-flow value materially off the net asset value is a signal to investigate, not a coincidence to ignore.
- Point the engine's exit-multiple terminal slot at an earnings-before-interest-tax-depreciation-and-amortization proxy you construct as net operating income less general and administrative expense, and set the multiple you apply to it as the reciprocal of your chosen exit capitalization rate, stating plainly in the rationale that the number is a capitalization rate expressed as a multiple, not a trading multiple read off comparable earnings. Treat the perpetuity growth path as the secondary, cross-check terminal — see Terminal below for why and how the two should reconcile.
- The mandatory distribution of taxable income means these companies cannot fund growth from retained cash flow the way an ordinary operating business can. External debt and equity issuance is structural, not opportunistic financing. Track diluted share count growth explicitly against the development and acquisition pipeline, and state whether new equity is priced above or below net asset value per share — that comparison, not the enterprise-level growth rate, determines whether the growth is accretive or dilutive to the per-share number the valuation ultimately reports. An enterprise-value forecast that never revisits share count hides exactly the judgment that decides whether growth helps the shareholder.
- State the whole substitution up front, before writing a single formula: that net operating income and adjusted funds from operations, not net income, anchor the chain; where the exit capitalization rate came from and that the terminal multiple is a capitalization rate in disguise; and whether any taxable-subsidiary income is broken out and separately taxed. A reader who sees only the numbers, not this paragraph, will otherwise read the chain as a mis-specified operating model rather than a deliberately reshaped real-estate one.

## Revenue — the decomposition that pays

- Decompose revenue as in-place rent times occupied square footage (or, for towers, revenue per tenant per site), with growth arriving through three separate mechanisms: contractual escalators on in-place leases, releasing spreads between expiring and newly signed rent on the same space, and occupancy change. A single blended revenue growth rate throws away the one disclosure that matters most for this sector — the lease expiry schedule — so build the forecast off it rather than off a top-line trend.
- Separate same-store net operating income growth (the existing portfolio, held constant) from external growth through acquisition and development. The two carry completely different capital costs and only same-store growth is repeatable without fresh capital; blending them into one growth number credits organic economics with growth that was actually purchased.
- For any active development pipeline, compare the development yield on cost (stabilized net operating income divided by total project cost) against the market capitalization rate for comparable stabilized assets. A yield meaningfully above the market cap rate is value creation from development; a yield near or below it says the company is paying market price for the privilege of construction risk, and the forecast should not credit it as if development were free alpha.
- Property types differ enormously in lease length, and that difference is the widest structural spread in this sector series: self-storage reprices monthly against a market rate, residential leases run about a year, office and industrial run several years, and net-lease retail and towers run on contracts spanning a decade or more. Use lease length as the organizing lens for every other judgment below — it sets inflation protection, cyclicality, and how much of next year's revenue is already locked in before you forecast a single assumption.
- Mall and shopping-centre leases commonly add percentage or overage rent — additional rent tied to a tenant's own sales once a sales threshold is cleared — on top of base rent and its escalator. That component ties part of the revenue forecast to retail sales performance at the property, not to the landlord's own leasing activity, and it should be forecast against a sales-growth assumption rather than folded into the base-rent escalator.
- Straight-line rent recognizes escalating contractual rent evenly over the lease term, so reported revenue in the early years of a long lease runs ahead of the cash actually collected. Treat the straight-line adjustment as its own line, not a component of cash revenue growth, and strip it before judging margin trend or cash conversion.
- Joint ventures and non-controlling interests are common structural features here, not edge cases: a meaningful share of net operating income, debt, and development activity can sit in vehicles the consolidated statements only partly reflect. Build the issuer's economic (look-through) share explicitly wherever it is disclosed rather than reading consolidated figures at face value.
- Data centres do not decompose as square footage at all: capacity is leased and billed in critical information-technology load (megawatts), so the occupancy metric is leased-versus-billed power, not floor area, and the escalator regime is tied partly to power cost pass-through rather than a flat contractual bump. Forecasting a data-centre issuer off a square-footage rent roll misreads the actual unit of sale.
- Towers decompose as tenants per site (colocation) and lease amendments (added equipment on an existing lease), not occupancy or square footage — a tower's "same-store" growth is really an escalator-plus-colocation growth rate against a largely fixed site count, and the forecast should be built on tenancy ratio and amendment activity, not a leasing-spread mechanism borrowed from an office or retail model.
- Self-storage prices on a monthly asking rate actively managed against local supply, closer to a hotel's revenue-management cycle than to a long-lease landlord's — existing-customer rent and new-customer street rate move separately and often in opposite directions, so a single blended rent-growth number can hide a new-customer rate war under a flat or rising existing-tenant average. Forecast the two rate streams separately where disclosed.

## Net operating income and the FFO/AFFO bridge — the level the story lives at

- Property operating expense — real estate taxes, insurance, utilities, repairs and property-level payroll — is the cost line this sector's story actually lives at, not a cost-of-revenue or operating-margin framing borrowed from a product business. Net operating income margin is set almost entirely by lease structure: a triple-net lease pushes nearly all property expense to the tenant and prices near the top of the margin range; a gross lease (most office, most apartments) leaves the landlord paying operating cost directly and margin sits far lower for an otherwise similar asset.
- General and administrative expense is corporate overhead sitting below net operating income, not inside it — forecast it as its own ratio to revenue or to net operating income, because it scales with platform size and management complexity, not with the property-level economics that drive the margin line above it.
- Build funds from operations and adjusted funds from operations as their own formula rows from the components in Chain shape above, and treat the ratio of adjusted to unadjusted funds from operations as a margin worth watching in its own right — a widening gap says recurring capital needs are rising faster than reported cash flow, which a bare funds-from-operations trend would miss entirely.
- Same-store net operating income margin drift over history is the cleanest read on whether pricing power or cost inflation is winning at the property level; a margin forecast that expands while property tax and insurance costs (the two lines with the least issuer control) are rising in the disclosures is a claim that needs a named offsetting cause.
- Healthcare splits into two structurally different economics under one property type: senior housing and skilled nursing leased to an operator on a triple-net basis behaves like contractual net-lease income, while the same property type run under an operating structure (the REIT bears occupancy and staffing risk through a taxable subsidiary and a third-party manager) behaves like a volatile, margin-driven operating business. Do not read one blended healthcare margin or growth rate across both structures within the same portfolio.
- Towers and, increasingly, data centres do not report a net operating income line by that name at all — towers report tower cash flow or an adjusted earnings-before-interest-tax-depreciation-and-amortization margin instead. Treat these as the same construction under a different label: property-level revenue less property-level operating cost, before the corporate overhead and financing lines. Do not read the different label as a different economic story.

## Reinvestment — recurring capital, tenant improvements and leasing commissions, not depreciation

The rule that binds every other sector in this series — capex converges to depreciation at steady state because a mature asset base is replaced, not expanded — does not apply here, and applying it produces a wrong terminal case. Book depreciation on real estate follows a tax and accounting schedule, not the asset's actual physical decay; a well-located, well-leased property can run decades past its depreciable life with rising, not falling, market value, so there is no economic reason for cash reinvestment to chase the depreciation number down or up to meet it.

- The real, non-optional reinvestment is recurring maintenance capital plus tenant improvements and leasing commissions on renewing and re-leasing space — the funds-from-operations-to-adjusted-funds-from-operations bridge, not a capex-to-depreciation ratio. Build it as a share of net operating income, and expect it to vary by lease length: short-lease sectors re-lease space constantly and carry a high recurring load; long-lease, triple-net sectors push tenant-improvement cost onto the tenant and carry almost none.
- Development and redevelopment capital is a separate, discretionary growth-capital story with its own funding and its own yield-on-cost discipline (see Revenue above) — do not blend it into the recurring capital ratio, and do not fade it toward a depreciation-based endpoint; fade it toward zero as a disclosed pipeline completes, or hold it at a through-cycle share of net operating income if the issuer is a continuous developer.
- Data-centre and tower reinvestment includes power, cooling and equipment upgrades that are more cyclical than a typical property type's recurring capital line, tracking the pace of technology refresh among tenants rather than a steady lease-driven rhythm; a flat recurring-capital ratio understates the spend during an active upgrade cycle and overstates it between cycles.
- Still build depreciation and amortization explicitly for the funds-from-operations addback and for the tax line — it is real for both those purposes — but never use it as the target a capex ratio should converge to. That parity check, load-bearing everywhere else in this series, is the wrong test here.
- Lodging is the one property type where the exception has an exception: furniture, fixtures and equipment reserves (typically several percent of hotel revenue) fund replacement of assets that genuinely wear out on a predictable schedule, so hotel capital spending sits closer to a real physical-maintenance story than any other property type in this sector. Do not apply the "depreciation is not economic" framing to the furniture-and-equipment reserve line the way it correctly applies to the building itself.

## Net asset value — the cross-check working capital cannot give you

Operating working capital is structurally near zero for a REIT: rent is collected monthly, there is no inventory, and payables are small relative to the asset base. The two items worth naming are tenant security deposits, which behave as a financing liability rather than a cash-conversion swing, and the straight-line rent receivable, which is the non-cash revenue item from Revenue above sitting on the balance sheet. Neither deserves the days-of-receivable treatment a product business gets.

The real discipline that belongs at this point in the chain is the net asset value cross-check built in Chain shape: forward net operating income capitalized at a market rate, less net debt, preferred and non-controlling claims. Because the underlying assets trade in an active transaction market, net asset value is an independent, non-model estimate of what the portfolio is worth today — read the discounted-cash-flow answer against it every time, and when the two diverge materially, trace the gap to a specific assumption (the exit cap rate, the terminal growth rate, or a same-store growth path inconsistent with current transaction cap rates) rather than treating either number as automatically right.

Source the market capitalization rate the same cross-check depends on from recent comparable transactions and, where the issuer discloses one, its own supplemental-package appraisal cap rate by property type — not from the issuer's own implied trading multiple, which embeds the market's view of management and growth on top of the real estate itself and is exactly what the cross-check exists to test independently.

## Tax and WACC notes

- The REIT distribution requirement pushes the effective corporate tax rate to near zero for REIT-qualified income; only income earned through a taxable subsidiary (common for hotel operations and some ancillary services) faces ordinary corporate tax, and that share should be forecast and taxed separately rather than blended into one consolidated rate.
- Lodging and much of senior-housing healthcare must legally run through this structure: the REIT cannot operate a hotel or an assisted-living community directly, so it leases the property to a taxable subsidiary that hires a third-party operator. That taxable slice carries ordinary operating leverage and ordinary corporate tax, and it sits inside net operating income figures that otherwise read as if nothing below the property line were taxed at all — separate it rather than taxing the whole portfolio at the near-zero rate that only applies to the directly-owned, REIT-qualified income.
- Near-zero corporate tax also means the debt tax shield in the discount-rate build is much smaller than it would be for an equivalent operating company: a weighted average cost of capital built with the full statutory tax adjustment on cost of debt overstates the shield and understates the discount rate. Set the effective tax rate input at the near-zero level the distribution requirement actually produces, not toward the statutory rate.
- This is the most rate-sensitive sector in this series, and through two separate channels: the exit and market capitalization rate moves with the general level of long-term rates and with real estate risk premia, and the cost of debt moves with it independently because most of the balance sheet is mortgage or unsecured bond financing. Do not let one rate assumption implicitly stand in for both channels — they can and do move by different amounts in the same environment.
- Fixed-rate debt maturity is a dated, forecastable event, not an average cost to hold flat. Read the debt maturity ladder from the filings and model each tranche refinancing at its own maturity date against a forward rate assumption, rather than blending the whole balance sheet into one weighted average cost of debt that never changes.
- Secured mortgage debt against a specific property and unsecured corporate bonds price differently and carry different refinancing risk; an issuer that shifts its mix between the two over the forecast is changing its cost of debt for a reason the maturity ladder alone will not show, so read the financing note for which mix a given maturity is expected to roll into.
- Beta and cost of equity vary with growth orientation and lease length as much as with leverage: a bond-like, long-lease net-lease or healthcare triple-net portfolio sits at the low end; a data-centre, tower or actively developing industrial platform sits meaningfully higher because its cash flows carry more growth and more re-leasing risk. Do not apply one REIT beta across an issuer whose portfolio spans both.
- Keep the joint-venture look-through consistent between the earnings side and the leverage side of the model: a net-debt-to-earnings ratio that includes consolidated joint-venture debt at one hundred percent while measuring earnings only at the issuer's economic share overstates leverage, and the reverse understates it. Use the same ownership share throughout.

## Terminal — what steady state means here

Terminal value here is a claim about what a buyer would pay for the portfolio at the end of the forecast, not a claim about a cash-flow growth rate compounding forever. That is exactly why an exit capitalization rate applied to forward net operating income is the right terminal construction and a perpetuity growth rate pulled from long-run gross domestic product is the wrong primary anchor: the sector's own market prices assets on a capitalization rate every day, and a terminal value should be read the way the market actually reads one.

- Set the exit capitalization rate modestly above the entry or current market cap rate — roughly fifty to one hundred fifty basis points wider — to reflect asset ageing: the portfolio at the terminal year is older, has absorbed more re-leasing cycles, and no longer carries whatever development-stage or newly-stabilized premium supported a tighter rate today. A terminal cap rate equal to or tighter than today's asserts the portfolio gets younger, which nothing in the chain supports.
- The engine offers a perpetuity growth rate and an exit multiple side by side. Use the exit-multiple slot as the primary terminal, built as described in Chain shape — an earnings-before-interest-tax-depreciation-and-amortization proxy from net operating income less general and administrative expense, multiplied by the reciprocal of the exit cap rate. Use the perpetuity path as the cross-check, capped near the portfolio's long-run contractual escalator rate rather than at the gross-domestic-product ceiling, because a REIT's organic growth ceiling is set by what its leases actually allow it to raise rent by, which is usually below nominal economic growth for long-lease property types and can sit above it only for short-lease types repricing to a strong market.
- Reconcile the two: back out the capitalization rate the perpetuity-growth terminal value implies (terminal-year net operating income divided by the terminal value it produces) and compare it with the exit cap rate you set directly. A material gap is a claim about the relationship between the discount rate, the growth rate and the market cap rate that you have not yet named — resolve it rather than reporting two unreconciled terminal values.
- Terminal growth still cannot exceed long-run nominal economic growth; nothing in real estate escapes that ceiling forever, even where near-term escalators run above it.
- Lodging carries its own cyclicality on top of the lease-driven cycle every other property type follows: revenue per available room moves with a multi-year travel and business-spending cycle, so a terminal year anchored on a peak or trough occupancy-and-rate combination inherits whichever half of that cycle the forecast happened to end in. Normalize to a mid-cycle revenue-per-available-room level before capitalizing it, the same discipline a cyclical industrial or semiconductor forecast owes its own cycle.

## Terminal-state calibration bands

These bands describe the steady state only — the economics a portfolio settles into once the current lease-expiry wave, development pipeline and rate cycle are behind it. They say nothing about next year and must not be used to set a near-term assumption: the explicit years are anchored on this issuer's own lease expiry schedule, portfolio disclosures and the causal case you built for them in Revenue above, and a sector band sitting beside that evidence would compete with it and win for the wrong reasons. Terminal is the one place the issuer's own history cannot speak, because it is by construction the state after the lease cycle that history covers has rolled over. Read the table as triage: a terminal input inside its band needs the ordinary rationale; one outside it is a claim that this issuer's steady state differs from its peers', and that claim needs evidence naming what makes the difference durable. Picking the midpoint because it is the midpoint is the treatment this paragraph warns against.

The reinvestment row is recurring maintenance capital plus tenant improvements and leasing commissions as a share of net operating income, not capex against depreciation — see Reinvestment above for why the usual parity check does not bind here.

## Failure modes specific to this sector

- **A capex-to-depreciation parity check applied at the terminal year.** This sector's reinvestment story is recurring capital plus tenant improvements and leasing commissions against net operating income; forcing capex toward the depreciation schedule invents a maintenance need the business does not have, or hides the one it does.
- **Net income or book value used as the valuation anchor.** Both are dominated by a depreciation schedule that does not track the assets' actual economics; a margin or return calculation built on either reads as either far too low or, once large gains on sale hit, far too high.
- **A blended revenue growth rate standing in for the lease expiry schedule.** Escalators, releasing spreads and occupancy change persist for different reasons and on different timelines; collapsing them into one number discards the one disclosure — the expiry schedule — that tells you what next year's revenue is already contractually close to.
- **Same-store and acquisition-driven growth forecast as one line.** They carry different capital costs and only one is repeatable without fresh capital; blending them credits the organic portfolio with growth that was purchased, and overstates what the existing asset base can deliver on its own.
- **A discounted-cash-flow value never checked against net asset value.** Real estate trades in an observable market; a model that never reconciles its answer to what the same portfolio would fetch in a transaction has skipped the one cross-check this sector's assets make available and nowhere else in this series is available at all.
- **Growth modeled as if it self-funds from retained cash flow.** The distribution requirement makes that structurally impossible; a forecast that grows the portfolio without growing share count, debt, or both has invented free capital, and per-share value depends entirely on whether that capital was raised above or below net asset value.
- **A full statutory tax shield applied to the cost of debt.** Near-zero corporate tax means the shield is worth far less here than in a similarly levered operating company; carrying the standard tax adjustment through the discount-rate build overstates the benefit of debt and understates the rate.
- **One net operating income margin or exit cap rate applied across a mixed-property-type portfolio.** Lease structure, not property quality, sets margin and cap rate; a diversified issuer spanning net lease and gross-lease property types needs the split, not a blended average that is wrong for both halves of the portfolio.
- **A real estate services or brokerage business built on this chain.** Fee-based property and investment management income behaves like an ordinary operating business — real capex, real working capital, real GAAP earnings — and belongs on the generic chain, not on a net-operating-income-and-cap-rate framework built for asset owners.
- **Square-footage occupancy applied to towers or data centres.** Neither sells space the way an office or a warehouse does — towers sell colocation slots on a largely fixed site count, and data centres sell power capacity — and a forecast built on floor-area occupancy for either misreads the unit of sale entirely.
- **A lodging terminal year anchored on a cyclical peak or trough.** Revenue per available room runs its own multi-year cycle independent of the lease-expiry cycle every other property type follows; capitalizing an unnormalized peak or trough year hands the terminal value whichever half of the travel cycle the forecast happened to end in.
- **Taxable-subsidiary income blended into the near-zero REIT tax rate.** Lodging and operator-run senior housing legally route income through a taxable subsidiary and a third-party operator; taxing that slice at the REIT's near-zero consolidated rate understates the tax the business actually pays and overstates after-tax cash flow.
- **A net asset value cap rate read off the issuer's own trading multiple.** That collapses the cross-check into the thing it exists to test independently — any comparison of the discounted-cash-flow answer against a self-referential net asset value will agree by construction, not by evidence.
- **Percentage rent smoothed into the base-rent escalator.** Mall and shopping-centre overage rent tracks tenant sales, not the landlord's contractual bumps; folding it into a single blended rent-growth number hides a swing factor that moves with retail sales performance, not with lease structure.
