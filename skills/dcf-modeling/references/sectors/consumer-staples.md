# Sector playbook — Consumer Staples

Covers three GICS industry groups: Food, Beverage & Tobacco (KO, PEP, PM, MO, MDLZ, GIS, KHC, STZ), Household & Personal Products (PG, CL, KMB, EL, CHD), and Consumer Staples Distribution & Retail (COST, WMT, KR, DG, SYY). The structural fact that breaks the generic margin-driven chain: unit volume is close to fixed by nature — the population eats, washes and smokes at a rate that does not scale with GDP — so almost all revenue growth this sector can durably claim is price and mix, and a chain that forecasts one blended revenue-growth rate is asserting an elasticity story it never tested. FCFF fits this sector without modification; there is no capitalized-reinvestment or deferred-revenue distortion to correct for, unlike technology or media.

## Chain shape — what to build instead of the default

- Replace a single revenue-growth assumption with a price/mix line and a volume line, forecast separately, for every material stream. This is the whole difference from the generic chain — everything else below follows from keeping the two apart.
- Identify which of the three business models an issuer runs before writing a cost assumption: brand manufacturer (COGS-and-A&P economics), tobacco manufacturer (excise-tax and litigation economics), or distributor/retailer (thin-margin, high-turnover economics). A diversified issuer routes each segment through its own logic below.
- Separate organic growth from acquisitions and currency translation before forecasting anything. Staples multinationals earn a large share of revenue abroad (KO, PM, CL, EL), so reported growth and the underlying unit economics can point in opposite directions in a single translation year.
- Split any driver row that a sub-industry shift would falsify: a household-products issuer with a meaningful prestige-beauty segment, or a food conglomerate spanning packaged food and beverage-alcohol, needs its margin and working-capital rows split by sub-industry rather than one consolidated ratio standing in for two different businesses.

## Revenue — the decomposition that pays

### Packaged food & beverage

Use the issuer's own organic-growth bridge (price/mix vs. volume/mix, ex-FX, ex-M&A) as the historical anchor; do not rebuild it from reported revenue alone. A multi-year record of price up and volume down is the single most diagnostic pattern in this sub-industry — it means the brand is monetizing inelastic demand, and that has a limit. Judge where the limit sits from the gap to private-label price, share-of-category trend, and whether volume decay is accelerating: a widening price gap against decelerating-but-still-negative volume is past the point where "price persists, volume stabilizes" is a free assumption. A concentrate/franchise model (KO) forecasts off case volume and concentrate pricing to bottlers, which is a different economic chain from an integrated manufacturer that also owns distribution (PEP) — do not apply one revenue mechanism to both.

Spirits and wine issuers (STZ and comparable beverage-alcohol portfolios) monetize mix through premiumization — shifting the portfolio toward higher-priced tiers — rather than through list-price increases on a fixed set of products; forecast the tier mix shift as its own driver, because it moves gross margin as well as revenue and a blended price/mix number hides which of the two is doing the work.

### Tobacco

Combustible cigarette volume declines at a knowable secular rate and pricing runs well above that decline in percentage terms — forecast the two as separate lines, never as one blended revenue growth, because they persist for entirely different reasons and a regulatory shock hits only one of them. Next-generation products (heated tobacco, oral nicotine, vapor) are a separate high-growth stream with its own adoption curve and margin profile; blending them into combustible price/mix hides the transition that is this sub-industry's actual growth story. A menthol or flavor ban, a flavor-cartridge restriction, or an excise-tax jump is a volume-line event, not a margin-line event — route it accordingly.

### Household & personal products

Same price/mix-versus-volume discipline as packaged food, with one addition: premium and prestige lines (skincare, fragrance) carry materially different elasticity and channel mix — DTC, travel retail, department store — than mass-channel staples, so decompose by channel or brand tier where disclosed rather than reading one blended growth number for a portfolio spanning both. Claims-based innovation (a reformulation with a demonstrable superiority claim) is this sub-industry's mechanism for raising price without losing volume; distinguish it from a plain list-price increase in the rationale, because the two carry different durability into next year.

### Distribution & retail

Decompose into comparable-store sales (traffic times ticket) plus net new unit growth, and — wherever the format sells memberships — membership fee income as its own line, never blended into merchandise revenue growth; the two have unrelated economics and margins (see below). Rising private-label penetration inside flat total comp sales is a share shift, not incremental volume: it is margin-positive for the retailer and simultaneously the volume headwind sitting on the other side of the branded manufacturers' forecasts above.

## Cost and margin — the level the story lives at

Input-cost pass-through with a lag is the sector's core margin mechanism for every manufacturer sub-industry: commodity and packaging costs (agricultural inputs, resin, aluminum, pulp) move first, list pricing follows a couple of quarters later, so gross margin compresses on the spike and recovers as price realization catches up. A chain that holds gross margin flat through a disclosed commodity move is asserting pass-through is instantaneous; it is not, and hedging programmes shift the timing of the compression, not whether it happens.

### Packaged food & beverage

Advertising and promotion is the discretionary lever management pulls to protect a near-term earnings number — a margin forecast built on cutting A&P as a share of revenue is borrowing against the brand equity that funds next year's price/mix. Check whether reported revenue is net or gross of trade promotion and slotting allowances: "growth" that is really a shrinking trade-spend contra-revenue line nets out very differently from volume growth, and the two need different rationales.

### Tobacco

Excise-tax increases pass through to price almost fully and almost immediately, unlike commodity cost inflation — treat a tax increase as a price-line event with a margin impact near zero, not as a margin risk, unless the jurisdiction caps retail price increases. Master Settlement Agreement payments and litigation reserves are quasi-fixed cash costs set by legal history, not by volume; model them as their own line rather than folding them into a volume-scaled cost ratio.

### Household & personal products

Same commodity pass-through lag as packaged food, on a different input basket (surfactants, resins, packaging). At maturity the margin lever is SG&A leverage from marketing efficiency rather than gross-margin expansion alone — split SG&A into brand-building spend and fixed overhead before forecasting either, because only one of them scales down with maturity.

### Distribution & retail

Gross margin here is a pricing policy chosen to drive traffic and volume, not a cost-structure outcome — do not forecast it off input costs the way a manufacturer's gross margin is forecast; forecast it off the issuer's stated pricing strategy and mix (private label share, general merchandise vs. grocery mix). The profit story lives in SG&A leverage over comparable sales on top of that thin gross margin, plus, where a membership format exists, fee income booked at close to 100% incremental margin: a warehouse club earns the large majority of its operating profit from membership fees, not from merchandise, so a merchandise-margin-only forecast misses the business. Model fee income and merchandise operating income as two separate streams for any membership-format issuer.

## Reinvestment — capex, D&A, and what maintenance means here

Free-cash-flow conversion is high and reinvestment intensity is low across all three sub-industries relative to the wider market — there is no expensed-R&D distortion of the kind that understates capex elsewhere; capex here means what it says (manufacturing lines, packaging capacity, distribution centers, store builds). The sector's real growth-reinvestment channel sits outside capex entirely: bolt-on brand acquisitions, funded by free cash flow or debt, land in goodwill and intangibles, not PP&E. A chain that holds capex flat while the causal case still assumes continued category-leading growth is quietly asserting that growth without naming what pays for it — either model disclosed, recurring bolt-on M&A as an explicit cash outflow with a return assumption, or keep the growth case organic.

For distribution and retail, separate maintenance capex (remodel cadence on the existing store base) from growth capex (net new unit openings): a mature chain's new-unit capex fades toward zero as the format saturates domestically while remodel capex persists indefinitely, and collapsing the two into one ratio hides which is actually declining. A multi-year e-commerce fulfillment and supply-chain buildout is a named, dated programme layered on top of that baseline, the same way a data-center build sits on top of baseline capex elsewhere — track it against its own disclosed trajectory and fade it toward the steady-state band once the network is built out, rather than treating an elevated build-phase ratio as the new normal. For brand-acquisitive issuers in any sub-industry, purchased-brand intangible amortization can be a large share of D&A with no capex counterpart; strip it before comparing capex to D&A for a parity check, or the comparison reads as under-investment that is really just deal accounting.

Tobacco is the most asset-light sub-industry in the sector: manufacturing a combustible cigarette requires little capacity investment relative to the cash it throws off, so a low reinvestment intensity here is not evidence of underinvestment the way it might be read elsewhere — check instead whether next-generation-product manufacturing capacity (which is genuinely capital-intensive during the ramp) is being funded, since that is where this sub-industry's real capex event sits.

## Working capital — the cycle and its sign

Packaged food, beverage, and household & personal products run working capital near zero or modestly negative, funded by extended supplier payment terms that many of these issuers manage as a deliberate treasury discipline — read a durably negative or low level as operating policy, not distress. Tobacco is the exception inside the manufacturer group: leaf tobacco curing and aging inventory turns slowly and keeps inventory days structurally elevated, so transplanting a packaged-food low-or-negative working-capital assumption onto a tobacco issuer misreads its actual cash-conversion cycle. Spirits and wine issuers sit alongside tobacco for the same physical reason: whiskey, cognac, and vintage wine held in barrel or bottle for years before sale keep inventory days elevated in a way no promotional or payment-terms discipline can offset — a maturing product mix (more aged, premium expressions) pushes the working-capital level higher still, not lower. Distribution and retail run structurally negative working capital — inventory turns faster than the terms extended by suppliers, so the format is financed by supplier float and comparable-sales growth is a source of cash rather than a use; confirm which regime an issuer sits in before defaulting to working capital scaling positively with revenue, the assumption that fits most other sectors.

## Tax and WACC notes

Effective tax rates sit close to the U.S. statutory rate for domestically-weighted issuers; multinationals with a large international revenue share (KO, the post-spinoff entirely-ex-U.S. PM, PG, CL, EL) run a few points below it on foreign earnings mix rather than on the IP-domiciling structures that depress technology-sector rates — check the issuer's geographic footprint before assuming either pattern. Do not read a below-statutory effective rate here as a structure at risk from the same global-minimum-tax regime that targets IP-domiciling in technology; a staples multinational's lower rate typically comes from where it manufactures and sells, not from where it books royalties, and that distinction determines whether the rate is durable. Leverage tolerance is high relative to the operating risk of the business: cash-flow stability lets these issuers carry more net debt/EBITDA than the operating volatility alone would justify, and that is priced-in capital structure flowing through the WACC weights, not a signal of distress to penalize in cost of equity. Beta is the lowest of nearly any sector for branded, non-cyclical packaged food, beverage, and household names — steady demand regardless of the cycle is the whole investment case. Tobacco and distribution/retail sit meaningfully higher: tobacco for regulatory and litigation tail risk layered on top of a secularly declining core product, distribution/retail for operating leverage on a razor-thin margin that amplifies comparable-sales swings into profit swings. Do not apply one sector beta across an issuer spanning branded manufacturing and distribution. Note also that PM's entirely-ex-U.S. footprint changes both its currency exposure and its regulatory-risk set relative to Altria's U.S.-only base — the two are not interchangeable comps despite a shared corporate history.

## Terminal — what steady state means here

The near-term causal case in this sector is usually built on a temporary phenomenon — a commodity cycle, an elasticity test, a next-generation-product ramp — so the terminal year should assume that cycle has averaged out, not that it has ended at its current phase. For tobacco specifically, terminal revenue growth can be positive, or roughly flat, even while terminal volume growth is negative: pricing power exceeding volume decline is the structural feature of this sub-industry at maturity, not a contradiction to resolve by forcing one line to match the other — state both explicitly so the terminal narrative does not read as internally inconsistent. For distribution and retail, terminal growth ties to a largely saturated domestic unit count plus low-single-digit comparable sales; an issuer still opening a significant number of net new units at the forecast horizon has not reached a steady state and the explicit forecast should extend until unit growth has genuinely slowed, and the same test applies to a still-scaling e-commerce fulfillment network: a chain still adding fulfillment capacity every year is still in its build phase, not its terminal state. Brand-acquisition-fueled growth cannot be extended into the terminal period as a distinct driver — perpetuating an M&A-assisted growth rate assumes an infinite supply of targets and infinite balance-sheet capacity to buy them; terminal growth should reflect organic economics only.

## Terminal-state calibration bands

These bands describe the steady state only — the economics the business settles into once the explicit forecast's price/mix cycle, litigation or regulatory event, product-transition ramp, or unit-growth programme has run its course. They say nothing about next year and must not be used to set a near-term assumption: the explicit years are anchored on this issuer's own history and the causal case built for it in Move 2, and a sector band competing with that evidence next to it would win for the wrong reasons. Terminal is the one place the issuer's own history cannot speak, because it is by construction the state after everything that history covers.

Read the table as triage, not as targets. A terminal assumption inside its band needs only the ordinary rationale; one outside it is a claim that this issuer's steady state differs from its sector's, and that claim needs evidence naming what makes the difference durable. Terminal capex and D&A converge to one steady-state reinvestment intensity — the row below — because a mature asset base is being replaced, not expanded; and terminal growth cannot exceed long-run nominal GDP.

## Failure modes specific to this sector

- **A blended revenue growth rate hiding a price/volume divergence.** One growth number can average a mid-single-digit price increase against a low-single-digit volume decline into a plausible-looking 2%, but the two persist for different reasons and face different limits — collapsing them loses exactly the signal that tells you where the elasticity ceiling sits.
- **Flat gross margin through a disclosed commodity move.** Holding gross margin constant while agricultural, resin, or packaging costs spike in the historical record asserts instantaneous pass-through; the honest chain shows compression followed by a lagged recovery as pricing catches up.
- **A packaged-food working-capital assumption applied to tobacco.** Leaf curing and aging inventory keeps tobacco working capital structurally positive; importing a negative-working-capital assumption from the rest of the sector understates the cash tied up in the business.
- **Merchandise-margin-only forecasting of a membership-format retailer.** Modeling only cost of goods and merchandise gross margin for a warehouse club or membership-driven grocer misses the stream — fee income — that generates most of its operating profit; the merchandise business exists partly to justify renewing the membership, not to maximize its own margin.
- **An M&A-fueled brand growth rate carried into the terminal period.** Extending a growth rate that was built on a multi-year run of bolt-on brand acquisitions into perpetuity assumes an endless supply of acquirable brands and endless balance-sheet capacity; terminal growth should reflect what the existing portfolio can do organically.
- **Treating PM and Altria as interchangeable comps.** They share a corporate history but not a footprint: one is entirely ex-U.S. with international regulatory and currency exposure, the other is U.S.-only — using one's multiple, margin, or growth profile to calibrate the other's terminal case transfers a risk profile that does not apply.
- **Reading an excise-tax increase as a margin risk.** Tax pass-through in tobacco is close to full and close to immediate, unlike commodity cost inflation; modeling it with the same lagged-margin-compression mechanism used for input costs overstates the near-term earnings hit and misses where the real risk sits (volume, not margin).
- **A premiumization mix shift modeled as flat unit pricing.** In spirits and wine, crediting margin expansion to broad price increases when the actual driver is a shift toward higher-tier, longer-aged products misprices the durability of the gain — a portfolio mix shift can reverse if the premium tier's growth slows, where a genuine list-price increase would not.
- **A build-phase e-commerce or digital-fulfillment capex ratio anchored as the terminal reinvestment level.** A distribution issuer's ratio during an active fulfillment-network buildout is elevated for a named, dated reason; carrying that level into the terminal calibration overstates steady-state reinvestment intensity once the network is complete.
