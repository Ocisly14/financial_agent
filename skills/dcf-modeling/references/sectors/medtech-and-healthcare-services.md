# Sector playbook — Medical Technology and Healthcare Services

Covers five GICS Health Care industries sharing a label and little else: Health Care Equipment (MDT, SYK, BSX, ABT, ISRG, EW), Life Sciences Tools & Services (TMO, DHR, A, WAT, IDXX), Managed Health Care (UNH, ELV, CVS, CI, HUM, CNC), Health Care Facilities (HCA, THC, UHS, DVA), and Health Care Distributors (MCK, COR, CAH). The structural fact common to all five: the person who uses the product or service is not the person who chooses and pays for it. A physician, hospital or plan sponsor decides; a payer — a commercial insurer, Medicare, Medicaid or a pharmacy benefit design — sets what the issuer actually collects. Revenue is therefore a function of reimbursement policy and payer mix first, patient demand second, and the issuer's own pricing power a distant third. Identify the sub-model before writing a single formula; the five diverge in what revenue is made of, what margin means, and in one case what the entire model is built from.

## Chain shape — what to build instead of the default

- Classify the issuer into one of the five sub-models below before touching revenue. A diversified issuer that straddles two (CVS runs managed care, pharmacy benefits and retail/provider clinics under one ticker) needs a chain that routes each piece to its own sub-model logic, summed — never one label applied to consolidated revenue.
- For managed care, do not build the margin-driven chain at all. Revenue is members times premium per member per month; the dominant cost line is the medical loss ratio, a regulatory floor on how little of premium the insurer may retain, not a cost ratio the issuer optimizes downward. Build member count, premium PMPM, and MLR as the three primary drivers, with the administrative expense ratio as the fourth; do not forecast "cost of revenue as a percent of revenue" as if this were a products business.
- For every other sub-model the margin-driven chain applies, but the level at which margin is forecast differs by business: gross margin at the product or segment level for devices and tools, admissions-and-rate for facilities, gross-profit-dollars-per-unit for distributors. Say explicitly which level you are forecasting at before writing the assumption.
- Payer mix (commercial vs. Medicare vs. Medicaid vs. self-pay) is a first-class driver wherever it appears — devices, facilities and managed care all reprice materially by payer — and belongs in the decomposition even when the issuer does not break out a clean segment for it.

## Revenue — the decomposition that pays

### Medical devices

Forecast an installed base of capital equipment separately from the consumables, disposables and service revenue attached to it: procedure volume times utilization per installed unit times price per procedure for the recurring stream, unit placements and replacement cycle for the capital stream. A single consolidated growth rate erases the razor-and-blade economics that make the business worth more than its revenue growth suggests. Price per procedure erodes most years from payer pressure and competitive bidding; volume and new-procedure adoption must outrun that erosion or the stream shrinks even as procedures grow.

### Life science tools and diagnostics

Split instrument (capital equipment) revenue from consumables and service revenue attached to the base. Instrument sales are cyclical and track customer capital budgets — pharma and biotech R&D spend, academic and government funding cycles — while consumables and service are recurring and track the installed base, not the funding cycle. The recurring share of total revenue is the single most consequential number in the model; state it explicitly and forecast the two streams on separate mechanisms. Several of these issuers carry a revenue base still distorted by pandemic-era testing and bioprocessing demand that has since receded; anchor the forecast off a normalized pre- or post-distortion base and say which periods you excluded and why, not off the peak or the trough year as reported.

### Managed care and health insurers

Revenue is membership by product line (commercial group, individual exchange, Medicare Advantage, Medicaid managed care) times premium per member per month. Forecast membership and PMPM separately per product line — they move on different mechanisms: membership on selling season and plan competitiveness, PMPM on medical cost trend and the administratively set government benchmark. Medicare Advantage benchmark rates and risk-adjustment factors and Medicaid capitation rates are set by CMS and state agencies on annual or multi-year cycles, not negotiated by the issuer the way commercial premium is; treat a rate-setting cycle as a named, dated event, not a smooth trend. For issuers with pharmacy or care-delivery arms (CVS, UNH's Optum, CI's pharmacy and care segments, HUM's CenterWell), forecast that revenue on its own mechanism — script volume and gross profit per script, or care-delivery revenue per patient — because none of it is underwriting and folding it into premium PMPM misstates both.

### Providers and facilities

Revenue is admissions or procedures (equivalent patient days, surgical cases, treatments for dialysis-type issuers) times realized reimbursement per case. Realized rate is set by payer mix — commercial pays several multiples of Medicare, Medicaid pays the least, self-pay collects worst — so forecast volume and payer mix as separate drivers, then apply each payer's own reimbursement trend to get blended rate. Bad debt and charity care are a structural deduction from gross revenue, not a cost line below it; a shift toward self-pay or uninsured volume shows up here before it shows up in margin.

### Distributors and pharmacy benefit managers

Revenue itself is close to meaningless for this sub-model — it is a pass-through of drug cost with a thin markup, and it can grow double digits on drug price inflation alone with zero change in the underlying business. Forecast unit or prescription volume and gross profit per unit or per script; let revenue fall out as a residual of volume times average drug price, and say so, or the growth line will read as the story when it is not.

## Cost and margin — the level the story lives at

### Medical devices

Forecast gross margin by product line or segment, not consolidated, wherever the issuer discloses enough to do so — a capital-equipment-heavy segment and a disposables-heavy segment carry structurally different gross margins, and a mix shift between them moves consolidated margin with no change in either segment's own economics. R&D and a large, largely fixed direct sales force are the two levers that separate operating margin from gross margin; a sales-force build tied to a new product launch is a named, temporary margin drag, not a permanent structural change.

### Life science tools and diagnostics

Gross margin blends a lower-margin instrument stream and a higher-margin consumables/service stream; forecast the blend from the mix, not as one flat ratio, especially when the recurring share is itself trending. These issuers are frequent acquirers, and purchased-intangible amortization sits inside cost of revenue or operating expense depending on issuer — check which, because it changes what "margin expansion" means and it fades as older deals age off.

### Managed care and health insurers

The medical loss ratio is the cost line, and it is bounded from below by regulation: ACA minimum-MLR rules (roughly 80% in individual and small-group, 85% in large-group and Medicaid managed care) force rebates to policyholders if the insurer retains too much of premium, so the margin ceiling is a legal fact, not a competitive outcome to model as improving indefinitely. Prior-period claims reserve development — favorable or adverse — flatters or damages a reported year exactly as loss-reserve development does at a property-casualty insurer; separate it from the current-year MLR before trending the ratio. The administrative expense ratio (selling, general and administrative cost as a share of premium) is the second driver and the one place operating leverage genuinely shows up. For the vertically integrated issuers, state what share of operating profit now comes from pharmacy and care-delivery arms rather than underwriting — that share carries product-business economics, not insurance economics, and consolidating them into one operating margin hides which engine is actually driving the number.

### Providers and facilities

Labor is the dominant cost and the primary margin swing factor — contract and travel nursing usage during shortages is the clearest single indicator of margin pressure, more informative than the aggregate wage line. Fixed costs (facility, equipment) mean margin is highly sensitive to volume and acuity; a same-facility admissions decline compresses margin faster than the revenue decline alone suggests because the cost base does not fall with it.

### Distributors and pharmacy benefit managers

Operating margin on revenue runs near one percent for the branded-and-generic distribution business; a margin-on-revenue forecast is arithmetically hopeless here and will not distinguish a healthy year from a deteriorating one. Forecast gross profit dollars per unit or per script and the operating cost to serve as its own line, then let the margin percentage fall out as a check, not a driver. Generic drug deflation and brand-to-generic conversions are a persistent tailwind to gross profit dollars even as they pressure revenue growth; say which effect you are forecasting.

## Reinvestment — capex, D&A, and what maintenance means here

Devices and tools issuers are moderately capital-intensive (manufacturing capacity, some capitalized software and internal-use IT) but the deeper reinvestment for devices is the direct sales force and clinical/regulatory spend embedded in operating expense, not capex — a capex ratio alone understates what it costs to defend share. Facilities are genuinely capital-intensive: real estate, imaging and surgical equipment, and IT systems require sustained capex close to or above D&A even in steady state, and deferring it shows up later as an aging asset base and lost volume to newer competitors. Distributors and PBMs are asset-light logistics and technology businesses; capex is a small fraction of revenue and D&A is correspondingly low — do not force a facilities-style reinvestment ratio onto a distributor. Managed care is the least capital-intensive of the five: reinvestment is mostly technology and network development, immaterial next to premium revenue, and the balance sheet's real "capital" is regulatory reserves and risk-based capital, not PP&E.

## Working capital — the cycle and its sign

Devices, tools and facilities run conventional positive operating working capital — inventory and receivables exceed payables — and facilities in particular carry long, payer-dependent collection cycles that lengthen when a payer mix shifts toward government programs or self-pay. Distributors run structurally negative working capital: they collect from customers on short terms while paying pharmaceutical manufacturers on longer terms, so growth in volume generates cash rather than consuming it — treat that float as a real, persistent part of the economics, not a rounding item. Managed care carries a working-capital-like mechanism even though it rarely appears as a conventional NWC row: premiums are collected in advance of the claims they fund, so growth in premium revenue is a source of cash through unearned premium and claims reserves, structurally similar to a subscription business's deferred revenue — say so explicitly if the mapped rows do not already carry it as a formal working-capital line.

## Tax and WACC notes

Effective tax rates across all five sub-models sit close to the US statutory rate — none of these business models routes profit through low-tax IP domiciles the way software or semiconductor issuers do, so a persistent large gap to statutory is unusual here and needs a named cause (an R&D credit mix, a state footprint) rather than being carried forward as a structural feature. Beta differs sharply by sub-model: managed care and providers have historically priced as defensive, demand-inelastic businesses with lower beta, though managed-care beta has risen with MLR and rate-setting volatility in recent cycles; devices and tools sit in the middle, insulated by recurring revenue but exposed to procedure-volume and capital-budget cycles; distributors sit low, since volume tracks drug utilization rather than the economic cycle. Do not apply one "healthcare beta" to an issuer that spans sub-models.

## Terminal — what steady state means here

For devices, terminal growth converges to population aging and procedure-volume growth net of ongoing price erosion — a durable single-digit real volume trend, not the elevated growth of a still-launching product cycle. For tools and diagnostics, terminal state assumes the recurring share has reached its ceiling and instrument-cycle volatility has been normalized away by capitalizing a mid-cycle capital-spending level, not the peak or trough of the most recent funding cycle. For managed care, terminal state is membership growth near overall population and government-program enrollment growth, an MLR sitting near its regulatory floor plus a stable spread, and an administrative ratio that has captured available scale economies — perpetual MLR compression is not a steady state, it is a regulatory violation waiting to happen. For facilities, terminal state is admissions growth near population and aging trends with a payer mix that has stopped shifting, and reinvestment at a level that sustains rather than expands the asset base. For distributors, terminal state is gross profit per script or per unit growing with drug price inflation and specialty-mix shift, with the razor-thin revenue margin unchanged — the business does not "mature" into a fatter margin.

## Terminal-state calibration bands

These bands describe steady state only — the economics this business settles into once the current product cycle, reimbursement change or utilization swing has fully played out. They say nothing about next year and must not set a near-term assumption: the explicit forecast years are anchored on this issuer's own history, disclosures and causal case, and a sector band placed beside that evidence would compete with it and win for the wrong reasons. Terminal is the one place the issuer's own history cannot speak, because it is by construction the state after everything the history covers. Read the table as triage — inside the band needs the ordinary rationale, outside it needs evidence naming what makes this issuer's steady state durably different, and the band itself never picks the number.

Terminal capex and D&A converge because a steady state replaces the asset base rather than expanding it; one reinvestment intensity per sub-model below is the level both approach, not two separate bands. Terminal growth cannot exceed long-run nominal GDP.

## Failure modes specific to this sector

- **Building managed care as a margin-on-revenue business.** Applying a growth-and-margin chain to a health insurer skips the actual mechanism entirely — members, PMPM and the MLR floor — and will not catch a rebate-triggering MLR breach or a benchmark rate cut, because neither shows up in a generic cost-ratio assumption.
- **A terminal MLR that keeps compressing.** Forecasting the medical loss ratio down as if it were an ordinary efficiency gain, past the regulatory floor, asserts the issuer escapes a rebate rule built explicitly to stop that.
- **A device or tools chain forecast on one consolidated growth rate.** Collapsing the capital-equipment stream and the consumables/service stream into one number erases the recurring-revenue story that is the actual investment thesis in this sub-model, in either direction.
- **A tools-sector base year still carrying pandemic-era demand.** Anchoring off a revenue level inflated by testing or bioprocessing volumes that have since receded compounds a distortion forward through every LAG-based formula in the chain.
- **A distributor modeled on operating margin instead of gross profit dollars.** At roughly one percent of revenue, small errors in the margin assumption swing the forecast by multiples of the real number; gross profit per unit is the only level at which the story is legible.
- **A facilities margin forecast with no labor-cost or payer-mix driver.** Margin at a hospital moves on wage inflation and payer mix far more than on volume alone; a chain that only forecasts admissions and a flat cost ratio misses the two variables that actually explain historical swings.
- **Ignoring which arm of a vertically integrated payer is producing the profit.** Consolidating underwriting, pharmacy and care-delivery income into one operating margin for CVS, UNH or CI hides that the growth and risk profile of each piece is different, and a single blended margin assumption cannot represent all three going forward.
- **A positive working-capital assumption forced onto a distributor or a growing insurer.** Both run structurally negative or cash-generative working capital by design; assuming NWC scales positively with revenue inverts a real cash source into a modeled cash use.
