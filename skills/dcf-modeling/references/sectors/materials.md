# Sector playbook — Materials (GICS Materials)

Covers Chemicals (LIN, SHW, DOW, LYB, APD, PPG, ECL), Metals & Mining (NEM, FCX, NUE, STLD, AA), Containers & Packaging (BALL, IP, AMCR, CCK, PKG) and Construction Materials (VMC, MLM). The structural fact that breaks the generic chain: for most of this sector, the selling price is set in a global commodity market the issuer does not control, so the forecast is a price-curve and cost-curve-position call, not a company pricing decision — margin comes from where the asset sits on the industry cost curve at whatever price the cycle delivers. Two sub-industries escape this: industrial gases and coatings within chemicals, and packaging converters, both of which run on contracted, cost-pass-through or formula-priced economics closer to industrials than to a commodity producer.

## Chain shape — what to build instead of the default

- Identify which economic regime the issuer sits in before writing a row: price-taker commodity producer (petrochemicals, base and precious metals, cement and aggregates spot exposure), pass-through converter (packaging), or contracted and formula-priced franchise (industrial gases, coatings, much of specialty chemicals). Each needs a different chain; do not apply one regime across a diversified issuer's segments — AA carries both spot alumina exposure and value-added product lines in the same filing.
- Build a normalized mid-cycle base year before writing any forecast formula. This is more consequential here than in almost any other sector; Revenue below says how.
- For chemicals, forecast the price-minus-feedstock spread as one linked story where the two are structurally tied, not revenue and cost of revenue independently.
- For mining and quarrying, decide the terminal structure before building the explicit chain: a perpetuity only holds where reserve life genuinely extends past the horizon at economic grades; otherwise build a finite life-of-mine schedule and cross-check it against net asset value. See Terminal below — this is the clearest case in the whole skill of an FCFF-perpetuity chain being the wrong instrument, and the alternative structure has to be decided up front, not patched in at stage 6.
- A diversified multi-commodity issuer (FCX: copper, gold and molybdenum; AA: bauxite, alumina and aluminum) needs a separate price deck per commodity, not one blended "metals price" assumption — the commodities do not move together, each has its own cost curve, and a single blended price hides which one is actually carrying the forecast.
- Where the issuer discloses hedges or fixed-price offtake contracts covering part of forecast volumes, forecast the hedged and unhedged volumes on separate price paths: the hedge locks a price for its tenor, and the exogenous-price story below applies only to what is unhedged.

## Revenue — the decomposition that pays

### Metals & mining, commodity chemicals, cement and aggregates spot exposure

- Decompose as volume times price and forecast the two independently: price is exogenous, set by the global market for the commodity; volume is the issuer's own production or shipment plan, constrained by capacity, permitting and, for mining, reserve grade.
- Never anchor price on the last actual year alone. Build a normalized realized price — a multi-year average spanning at least one full cycle, or a forward or futures curve where a liquid one exists (COMEX copper, gold forwards) — and state explicitly where in the cycle the anchor year sits relative to that normalized level.
- Volume growth is a capacity and permitting story, not a demand-extrapolation story: new capacity arrives in discrete, disclosed, dated increments — a quarry permit, a mine expansion, a plant debottleneck — not as a smooth trend line.
- Treatment and refining charges that a smelter deducts from a concentrate producer's realized metal price are a separate, annually negotiated benchmark, not a fixed spread — model the deduction as its own line rather than burying it inside one blended realized-price assumption; it moves independently of the metal price and compresses producer margin whenever smelter capacity runs short relative to concentrate supply.

### Chemicals — spreads

- Forecast product price and feedstock cost as one linked spread rather than two independent lines. Modeling them separately loses the correlation that actually governs profit: feedstock and product prices co-move with the same energy and demand cycle far more than either co-moves with GDP.
- Geographic feedstock advantage — US ethane cracking versus naphtha-based Asian or European crackers — is a durable structural asset, not a temporary spread. Carry a Gulf Coast producer's cost advantage forward unless a named new capacity wave (export gas demand lifting domestic feedstock costs, new crackers coming online globally) erodes it.
- Industrial gases (LIN, APD) and coatings (SHW, PPG, ECL) are the exception. On-site and pipeline gas contracts carry take-or-pay volumes and cost pass-through clauses; coatings and specialty formulations reprice through contractual index terms or annual price actions. Forecast these on contracted volume growth and a margin that persists through the commodity cycle, not on a spot spread mechanism — putting them on the same chain as a commodity petrochemical producer is a category error.

### Containers & packaging

- Forecast unit volume (cans, boxes, containers shipped) and the resin, containerboard or aluminum cost pass-through separately. Revenue tracks the pass-through with a lag, so an input-cost spike shows up in revenue before it shows up in margin pressure, and unwinds the same way.
- Growth follows end-market unit demand (beverage cans, e-commerce corrugated) and contract wins or losses, not commodity price — the commodity moves through the P&L, it does not drive the volume line.

### Construction materials — aggregates and cement

- Aggregates carry genuine local pricing power: a quarry is a local monopoly bounded by transport cost, since aggregates do not travel economically much beyond about fifty miles by truck. Price growth persists through a cycle even when volume does not — do not fade aggregate pricing toward the commodity-price assumption you would apply to copper or steel.
- Volume tracks regional construction activity — public infrastructure funding, non-residential, residential — and these run on different cycles, so decompose by end market where disclosed rather than treating construction demand as one line.

## Cost and margin — the level the story lives at

- Forecast unit cash cost per tonne, per pound or per ton, not a margin percentage, for any price-taker sub-industry — mining, commodity chemicals, cement clinker. Margin there is an exogenous price minus a cost the issuer controls; forecasting margin directly as a percentage of a moving price hides the real driver and makes a peak-price year and a trough-price year look like the same underlying business.
- Cost-curve position, not management skill, determines whether a margin survives a trough. A first-quartile-cost producer earns a positive cash margin through the bottom of a cycle that a fourth-quartile producer cannot. State which quartile this issuer occupies today and name anything that moves it — a shift into higher-grade ore, a disclosed cost program, a competitor's high-cost closure tightening the curve.
- Unit cash cost rises over a mine's life from grade decline, deeper pits and rising strip ratios, and from processing progressively lower-quality ore as the best material is extracted first. This happens with nothing going wrong operationally, so a flat or declining terminal unit-cost forecast needs a specific named cause — a mill expansion, a high-grade zone entering the mine plan — not general cost-discipline language.
- For chemicals, keep the margin story and the feedstock-advantage story separate: a US ethane cracker's margin can hold even as global spreads compress, because its cost basis, not its selling price, is what differs from an Asian naphtha cracker.
- Packaging margin is the most stable number in this sector because the pass-through contract, not the commodity cycle, sets it. Do not import the sector's commodity cyclicality into a converter's margin forecast — the margin story here is operating leverage on volume and mix (can-size and can-material mix, e-commerce box complexity), not commodity price exposure.
- Byproduct credits — silver or molybdenum credited against a copper mine's cost, for example — can turn a nominally high-cost primary product into a low net-cost producer. Forecast the byproduct's own price and volume and net it against primary unit cost explicitly, rather than folding it into one blended margin that hides which commodity's price is actually protecting the economics.

## Reinvestment — capex, D&A, and what maintenance means here

- Split growth capex from sustaining capex explicitly. Growth capex is a named, dated, disclosed project with a stated capacity number attached — a new cracker, a mine expansion, a new can line. Sustaining capex is what it costs to hold current capacity and, for a mine, current grade and strip ratio. Only sustaining capex belongs in the terminal convergence with depreciation — fading total capex toward depreciation at the horizon while a disclosed growth project is still ramping asserts the project finishes exactly at the horizon.
- Mines and quarries deplete. Reserve life is finite and disclosed as reserve tonnage divided by annual production rate. A perpetuity-growth terminal value implicitly assumes the reserve base is replaced forever through exploration or acquisition — if you use one, the reinvestment assumption must fund that replacement (historical finding-and-development cost per tonne or ounce is the anchor), not just sustaining capex on the pits already permitted. Where reserve life is short relative to a normal forecast-plus-terminal horizon, a perpetuity is the wrong terminal structure: build a life-of-mine schedule to reserve exhaustion, including closure and reclamation outflows, and cross-check it against a net-asset-value sum of discounted mine-level cash flows instead. State which structure you used and why.
- Aggregates and cement permitted-reserve life is typically far longer than an ore-mining reserve and is more often genuinely perpetuity-eligible — check the issuer's disclosed permitted reserve life before assuming the mining constraint applies.
- Packaging capex is line-and-plant capacity capex, not deposit-based — treat it like an industrial issuer's capacity spend, fading toward depreciation at maturity with no depletion consideration at all.
- Deferred stripping — waste-rock removal incurred to access ore that will be mined in a later phase — is capitalized as an asset and amortized against the ore it exposes, not expensed when incurred. Check whether the issuer capitalizes or expenses stripping: capitalizing it moves cash cost out of the current period and into a future year's depreciation, understating today's unit cost and overstating a later year's.

## Working capital — the cycle and its sign

- Inventory carried at production cost against revenue recognized at market price means operating working capital as a share of revenue mechanically compresses at a price peak and expands at a price trough, with no change in physical inventory management. Do not read that swing as a working-capital efficiency story — it is the price cycle passing through the balance sheet.
- Reclamation, asset-retirement and environmental remediation obligations are real future cash outflows tied to mine and plant closure. They belong in the equity bridge as a debt-like obligation at the anchor date, not folded into operating working capital or ignored because they sit inside a long-term-liability caption.
- Packaging working capital moves with the same resin or board cost pass-through that moves revenue: a swing in input cost inflates receivables and payables together and largely nets out. Do not model it as a pure use of cash the way a rising-cost mining inventory build should be.
- Mining assets are frequently held through a joint venture with a noncontrolling partner — a copper mine co-owned with a state entity or another producer, for example. Confirm whether reported revenue, cost and capex are consolidated at 100% or at the issuer's economic interest, and size the noncontrolling-interest bridge row on the same basis; a mismatch here misstates equity value by the partner's share of the asset.

## Tax and WACC notes

- Through-cycle beta runs high for commodity price-takers — mining and commodity chemicals — because the market prices the operating leverage onto an exogenous commodity price. A beta measured only over a recent up-cycle or down-cycle window understates the sector's true cyclicality; prefer the full ten-year window here specifically rather than a truncated recent one.
- Industrial gases, coatings and packaging carry materially lower beta than the rest of the sector because their cash flows are contracted or pass-through. Do not apply one "materials beta" across an issuer whose segments span both regimes.
- Effective tax rates in this sector track statutory rates more closely than in IP-light sectors, because a mine, quarry or cracker is tied to physical geography and there is little to domicile offshore. A persistent large gap to statutory needs a named cause — foreign mining royalty or tax-credit structures, net operating losses carried from a prior downturn — rather than being carried forward by default.
- Large capital projects — a new mine, a new cracker, a new can line — are frequently financed with project-level or asset-backed debt structured differently from corporate bonds. Confirm whether that project debt is already inside the total debt figure the WACC weights use, or sits in an unconsolidated joint venture, before taking a reported debt balance at face value.

## Terminal — what steady state means here

- Terminal-value off a normalized mid-cycle year, never off the final explicit-forecast year's actual price or margin. The terminal year is whatever point the explicit forecast happens to end on, and capitalizing a cycle peak or trough into perpetuity inherits the wrong half of the cycle by construction.
- For mining and quarrying, settle the terminal structure before touching growth or the exit multiple. Perpetuity growth is appropriate only where disclosed reserve life plus a defensible replacement assumption genuinely extends past the horizon; otherwise use a finite life-of-mine schedule to depletion, including closure costs, or a net-asset-value cross-check, and state explicitly which you used.
- Terminal growth for a price-taker commodity producer is a claim about long-run real commodity price appreciation plus volume growth bounded by permitted or economically viable capacity. It is rarely close to the GDP ceiling in practice, because commodity prices trend toward the long-run marginal cost of new supply, not toward nominal GDP.
- Read the exit multiple on mid-cycle EBITDA, never on a peak or trough final year's EBITDA. Quoting a peer multiple observed during a cycle peak against a peak-year EBITDA number double-counts the cycle — the multiple itself already compresses at the peak because the market is pricing the same reversion you would be capitalizing.
- A diversified multi-commodity issuer's terminal value is really the sum of terminal claims on assets with different reserve lives and different cost-curve positions. Do not average them into one blended terminal margin and growth rate — carry the mismatch through explicitly if one segment's mine life ends inside the valuation horizon and another's does not.

## Terminal-state calibration bands

These bands describe the steady state only — the economics the business settles into after the explicit forecast, once growth has normalized, the current build programme or product ramp is over, and, where relevant, the commodity price cycle has been normalized to its mid-cycle level. They say nothing about next year and must not be used to set a near-term assumption: the explicit years are anchored on this issuer's own history, disclosures and causal case, and a sector band that competed with that evidence would be the wrong master. Terminal is the one place the issuer's own history cannot speak, because it is by construction the state after everything the history covers.

Read the table as triage, not as a target: a terminal assumption inside its band is ordinary and still needs its own rationale; one outside it is a claim that this issuer's steady state differs from its sector's, and that claim needs evidence naming what makes it durable.

Two structural facts bind the table. Terminal capex and depreciation converge on one sustaining-reinvestment intensity — the level both approach — because steady state replaces the asset base rather than expanding it; growth capex has no place in a terminal row. And terminal growth cannot exceed long-run nominal GDP — for the price-taker sub-industries here it typically sits well below that ceiling, since commodity prices trend toward the marginal cost of new supply rather than toward GDP.

## Failure modes specific to this sector

- **A base year taken at a cycle price extreme.** A LAG-anchored formula propagates whatever price sat in the last actual period; a chain anchored on a commodity price spike or trough compounds it forward by multiples of the mid-cycle answer, not by percentage points off it. Normalize before anchoring, for every price-taker line, every time.
- **Margin forecast as a percentage instead of unit economics.** A margin percentage on a moving exogenous price hides whether the forecast is really a price call or a cost-position call; write unit cash cost and let margin fall out of price minus cost, so the two claims stay visible separately.
- **A mine or quarry perpetuity with no reserve check.** Setting terminal growth and letting the engine compound FCFF forever silently assumes reserves replace themselves forever at no disclosed cost. Check reserve life before choosing the terminal structure, and use a life-of-mine schedule or a net-asset-value cross-check where the perpetuity does not hold up.
- **Industrial gases or coatings put on a commodity chemicals chain.** Forecasting a name like LIN or SHW off a spot spread mechanism erases the contracted, formula-priced economics that are the entire reason those franchises earn a premium multiple — build them on contracted volume and a persistent margin instead.
- **Packaging inheriting the sector's cyclicality.** Fading a converter's margin with the same amplitude as a commodity producer's ignores that the pass-through contract, not the cycle, sets its economics — the failure runs in both directions, understating margin stability in normal years and overstating cyclical risk in stressed ones.
- **Reclamation and closure obligations left out of the bridge.** These are disclosed, real, future cash outflows tied to mine or plant closure; omitting them from the equity bridge because they sit inside a long-term-liability caption overstates equity value by exactly their discounted amount.
- **An exit multiple read off peak-EBITDA comparables.** Sourcing a peer EV/EBITDA multiple observed during a cycle peak and applying it to a peak-year EBITDA compounds the same cycle assumption twice — both the multiple and the metric it is applied to must be mid-cycle.
- **A joint-venture stake consolidated at the wrong basis.** Forecasting 100%-consolidated revenue and cost while sizing the noncontrolling-interest bridge row off the issuer's economic interest, or the reverse, misstates per-share value by the partner's share of the asset in every period.
- **TC/RCs buried inside a blended realized price.** A concentrate producer's treatment-and-refining deduction is set annually and moves independently of the metal price itself; folding it into one realized-price assumption hides a real, separately negotiated margin driver.
