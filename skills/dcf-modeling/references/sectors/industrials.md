# Sector playbook — Industrials

Covers Capital Goods (CAT, DE, HON, GE, EMR, ETN, PH), Aerospace & Defense (BA, RTX, LMT, GD, NOC, TDG, HEI), Transportation (UNP, CSX, UPS, FDX, DAL, UAL, ODFL), and Commercial & Professional Services (WM, RSG, VRSK, CTAS). The structural fact that breaks the generic margin-driven chain: these businesses carry high fixed cost against cyclical volume, so the number that forecasts the P&L is not the margin level but the **incremental margin** — the share of each additional revenue dollar that reaches operating profit — and its uglier twin, the decremental margin on the way down. A level-margin forecast through a volume swing silently asserts the cost base is variable; almost none of it is.

## Chain shape — what to build instead of the default

- Forecast the flow-through rate, not the margin level: build the incremental margin — the change in operating income divided by the change in revenue — as its own history row, judge whether it persists, and let margin fall out of applying it to your revenue forecast rather than asserting a margin target directly.
- Split every issuer into long-cycle or short-cycle before writing a single revenue formula. Long-cycle (aerospace programmes, power generation, rail and fleet equipment, large project-based capital goods) converts backlog into revenue on a schedule you can read off disclosed book-to-bill and backlog duration; short-cycle (components, distribution, hand tools, most aftermarket) turns with industrial production and order rates within a quarter or two. Blending the two into one revenue growth assumption erases the leading indicator either one actually offers.
- Where an issuer sells both original equipment and an attached service or parts stream, build them as two revenue streams with two margin stories from the start (see Revenue and Cost below) — collapsing them into one consolidated margin hides where the profit actually sits.
- Treat Defense as a distinct sub-model, not a cyclical capital-goods segment with a different customer: revenue follows appropriated budgets and programme awards, and margin follows contract type, both of which move on a political and legislative calendar, not an economic one.
- Treat Transportation as a distinct sub-model built on capacity × utilization × yield rather than a margin-driven chain at all; the P&L there is a byproduct of three physical/pricing drivers, not the other way around.

## Revenue — the decomposition that pays

### Capital goods and diversified machinery

- Decompose revenue into original equipment and aftermarket (parts, service contracts, rebuilds) before forecasting either — they are different cyclicality and different pricing power wearing one reporting line.
- Use backlog and book-to-bill (orders divided by shipments over the same period; above 1.0 signals building backlog) as the leading indicator for the OE stream over the next several quarters to a few years, longer for long-cycle names (power generation, large mining and construction equipment) than for short-cycle ones (hand tools, small components).
- Aftermarket revenue tracks the installed base in the field times an attach or service-intensity rate, and grows more steadily than OE because the base doesn't shrink as fast as new orders can.

### Aerospace & Defense

- Commercial aerospace splits the same way as capital goods, sharper: original equipment is frequently sold at a low margin or even near cost, because the OEM is buying decades of monopoly aftermarket revenue on that airframe or engine — spares, overhaul, and service contracts priced with far more power than the jet itself.
- Decompose OE deliveries (aircraft or engine unit count × price) from aftermarket (installed fleet size × flight-hour or cycle-based consumption × price), or the margin forecast is meaningless; a supplier whose OE mix is rising can show flat or falling consolidated margin even as the business gets structurally more valuable.
- Defense revenue is a function of appropriated budgets and programme awards, not GDP or industrial production — anchor it to disclosed programme backlog, funded vs. unfunded backlog, and the multi-year budget and procurement outlook, not to a macro growth rate.

### Transportation

- Build capacity × utilization × yield explicitly rather than a single growth rate; the P&L is a byproduct of these three drivers, not the other way around.
- Railroads: carload or unit volume × revenue per carload (or ton-miles × revenue per ton-mile), with network velocity and car utilization as the swing factor between a given volume and the revenue and cost it produces.
- Airlines: available seat miles (capacity) × load factor (utilization) × yield (revenue per passenger mile) — capacity is the airline's own growth decision (fleet and schedule), so forecast it as a company choice, not a market given.
- Parcel and less-than-truckload: package or shipment volume × weight or cube × revenue per unit, with density (volume per route-mile or per terminal) as the profitability lever — incremental volume over an already-built network is far more profitable than the average shipment, the transportation sector's version of the incremental-margin story above.

### Commercial and professional services

- Split recurring, contracted revenue (waste collection and disposal under multi-year municipal or commercial contracts, uniform and facility-services rental under evergreen contracts) from project or transactional revenue (special waste, one-time services).
- The recurring base carries built-in price escalators (often CPI-plus) and is the durable part of the story; forecast it off customer count, route density, and contracted pricing rather than a blended top-line growth rate.

## Cost and margin — the level the story lives at

- Forecast the incremental margin, not the margin level, and read management's own guidance on it — this sector is one of the few where issuers guide on flow-through explicitly.
- Decremental margins on the way down typically exceed incrementals on the way up: severance and restructuring costs land before volume actually falls, pricing given up to defend share doesn't come back symmetrically when volume returns, and fixed costs (a plant, a network, a landing slot) can't be shed at the same speed volume disappears.
- A downside case built on the same flow-through rate as the upside case understates the damage; use a wider, uglier decremental margin below trend revenue.
- Material and input costs (steel, aluminum, fuel, resin) are passed through with a lag via price increases or surcharges — model the pass-through mechanism and its lag explicitly rather than assuming margin absorbs commodity swings or recovers them instantly.
- In Defense, margin is a function of contract type, not volume: cost-plus contracts carry a low, capped, near-guaranteed fee (management-fee-type economics); fixed-price development contracts carry real loss risk, because a cost overrun on a fixed-price programme is recognized as a loss the moment the estimate at completion rises, well before delivery. A defense issuer's consolidated margin is a blend of its cost-plus/fixed-price and production/development mix, and that mix — not end-market demand — is what should move the forecast.
- In transportation, fuel is a large cost recovered with a lag through surcharges; labor, largely under multi-year union contracts at railroads and legacy airlines, is the other dominant cost and moves in discrete steps at contract renewal, not smoothly with volume.

## Reinvestment — capex, D&A, and what maintenance means here

- Separate maintenance capex from growth or fleet-renewal capex wherever the issuer discloses the split; in the most capital-intensive sub-industries here (railroads, airlines) maintenance capex is not discretionary — track renewal, equipment overhaul, and airworthiness-driven maintenance are regulatory and safety obligations that persist regardless of the cycle, so do not fade capex down as a lever to lift free cash flow in a downturn scenario.
- Capital goods and defense manufacturers are comparatively capex-light — plant and tooling rather than a transportation network — with R&D expensed through operating cost as the more relevant reinvestment line for OE competitiveness.
- Check whether facilities are government-owned-contractor-operated at defense primes, which understates the issuer's own capex relative to its production footprint.
- At waste and environmental services issuers, capex includes landfill airspace development — buying future disposal capacity is a real reinvestment with its own depletion economics, not a pure maintenance outlay, and belongs in the reinvestment story alongside the collection fleet.

## Working capital — the cycle and its sign

- Working capital swings hard with volume and, unlike the generic growth-consumes-cash default, becomes a source of cash in a downturn: inventory and receivables unwind as volume falls, releasing cash exactly when the P&L looks worst.
- Do not forecast operating working capital as a fixed share of revenue through a cycle you are also forecasting as cyclical — let the ratio move with the volume story, and expect it to fund the business on the way down.
- Customer advances and progress payments on long-cycle orders (aircraft, defense programmes, large machinery) are genuine float — cash collected ahead of cost incurred — and can make operating working capital structurally negative for a long-cycle issuer, the transportation-sector analog to deferred revenue elsewhere.
- Airlines run structurally negative working capital for the same reason from the other side: the air-traffic liability is cash collected for travel not yet flown.
- Railroads carry minimal inventory (fuel and parts) and close to zero working capital intensity.
- Pension and other post-employment obligations are large enough at legacy capital-goods and transportation issuers to matter materially in the equity bridge — treat as a bridge item, not as working capital.

## Tax and WACC notes

- Effective tax rates in this sector sit close to the US statutory rate plus state tax — there is little of the IP-domiciling structure that depresses effective rates elsewhere — so a terminal rate far below the low-20s% asserts a tax planning story this sector does not typically have.
- Beta spans an unusually wide range here and must be picked by sub-industry, not by the sector label: defense primes carry some of the lowest betas in the market because government-appropriated revenue is close to non-cyclical; environmental and contracted commercial services (waste collection, uniform rental) are similarly defensive; capital goods, railroads and logistics networks sit in the middle, moved by industrial production and freight volumes; airlines and cyclical short-cycle equipment suppliers carry the highest beta in the sector because operating leverage (fixed networks and fleets) compounds with financial leverage (debt-financed aircraft and equipment).
- Lease accounting matters more here than in most sectors — aircraft and much of the equipment and vehicle fleet in transportation and commercial services is leased, so read the filing's lease note before setting the bridge's lease treatment (see stage 6): a large finance-lease balance at an airline or trucking-heavy issuer is real debt, not an operating cost already captured above the operating-income line.

## Terminal — what steady state means here

- Terminal growth cannot be a continuation of a backlog conversion story — backlog is by construction a near-term, book-to-bill-driven phenomenon that has fully worked through by the horizon, so terminal revenue growth has to be re-grounded in unit volume growth and price, not in the backlog schedule that drove the explicit years.
- For a long-cycle issuer, say explicitly that the terminal case assumes replacement-level bookings continue at a steady book-to-bill near 1.0, because that is a different claim than "the current backlog keeps converting."
- For railroads, terminal growth is bounded not just by GDP but by freight's share of GDP, which has drifted down for decades as trucking captured share — a terminal growth rate at the GDP ceiling asserts that erosion stops.
- For defense, terminal growth is a claim about long-run appropriated budget growth continuing, which is a policy assumption, not an economic one — state it as such.
- Because this is a physically depreciating asset base almost everywhere in the sector, terminal capex and D&A converging to one steady-state reinvestment intensity is a real physical constraint here, not a modeling convenience: an issuer whose terminal capex sits durably below D&A is asserting its network or fleet shrinks.

## Terminal-state calibration bands

These bands describe the steady state the business settles into once the current cycle, backlog conversion or build programme is over and growth has normalized to trend — not next year. The explicit forecast years are anchored on this issuer's own history, disclosed backlog and causal case; a sector band placed next to that evidence would compete with it and win for the wrong reasons. Terminal is the one place the issuer's own history cannot speak, because it is by construction the period after everything that history covers, so a sector band earns its place only there. Read the table as triage: a terminal assumption inside its band is ordinary and gets the ordinary rationale; one outside it is a claim that this issuer's steady state differs from its sector's, and that claim needs evidence naming what makes it durable — the band never picks the number.

## Failure modes specific to this sector

- **A flat consolidated margin forecast through a volume swing.** Holding margin level while revenue moves materially asserts the cost base is variable; forecast the incremental or decremental margin and let the level fall out, or name explicitly which costs actually flex with volume.
- **Symmetric incrementals and decrementals.** Using the same flow-through rate for an upside and downside case ignores that severance, idle-capacity cost and price given up to defend share make the way down uglier than the way up was good.
- **Blending long-cycle and short-cycle revenue into one growth rate.** It erases the leading indicator each one actually offers — backlog conversion for one, industrial-production correlation for the other — and produces a forecast anchored on neither.
- **A single consolidated margin over an OE-plus-aftermarket business.** The OE unit can be sold near cost to plant an aftermarket annuity; a blended margin forecast hides whichever of the two is actually carrying the business and misreads a rising OE mix as margin deterioration when it may be a growing future annuity.
- **Defense modeled like commercial capital goods.** Applying an industrial-production or GDP-linked growth rate, or a through-cycle beta borrowed from commercial industrials, misreads a business whose revenue follows an appropriations calendar and whose margin follows contract type.
- **Capex faded down in a downturn scenario at a railroad or airline.** Track renewal and airworthiness-driven maintenance capex are not discretionary; a cash-flow upside built by cutting them is a safety and regulatory claim, not a financial one.
- **A terminal growth rate for a railroad set at the GDP ceiling.** It ignores the multi-decade share loss of rail-eligible freight to trucking, a trend distinct from the general economy's growth.
- **Working capital held flat as a share of revenue through a modeled cycle.** It contradicts the cyclicality already built into the revenue forecast — this sector releases cash on the way down, and a flat-ratio assumption erases exactly the swing the rest of the model asserts.
- **An EV/EBITDA comp read off an equity research note without checking the metric.** Airline and lease-heavy fleet comps are frequently quoted on EBITDAR; applying that multiple to this model's EBITDA overstates terminal value by the capitalized lease cost.
