# Sector playbook — Banks

Covers diversified and money-center banks, regional banks and thrifts (JPM, BAC, WFC, C, USB, PNC, TFC, FITB, MTB, RF), including universal banks whose markets and investment-banking arms sit beside the lending book. Custody banks (BK, STT, NTRS) belong here too: their balance sheet is small relative to fee revenue and net interest income is a byproduct of client cash balances rather than the main lending engine — flag that difference where it changes a number, not where it changes the mechanism. The structural fact that breaks the generic chain: for a bank, debt is raw material, not financing. Deposits and wholesale borrowings fund the asset side of a spread business, interest expense is a cost of production, and there is no capex, no inventory, and no operating working capital worth separating from the loan book itself. A bank has no enterprise value — it is valued as an equity claim directly, and every downstream section of this file follows from that one fact.

## Chain shape — what to build instead of the default

- Discard free cash flow to the firm. Build free cash flow to equity instead: net income available to common shareholders, less the common equity the balance sheet must retain to fund asset growth at the target capital ratio. Retained-equity need is this sector's reinvestment — see Capital below.
- Cross-check with a residual-income (excess-return) construction: value equals current tangible common equity plus the present value of the spread between return on tangible common equity and the cost of equity, applied to a growing equity base. The two constructions must converge on the same number when built from the same earnings and growth path — if they diverge, the gap names a modeling inconsistency, not a sector effect.
- Build both constructions from the same earnings, growth and capital-retention rows rather than as two independent models; the residual-income view is a lens on the same forecast, not a second forecast to reconcile after the fact.
- Discount at the cost of equity alone, never a debt-and-equity weighted rate. The engine's discount-rate sheet is built to blend a cost of debt and a tax shield into the one rate the model discounts at; a bank has no such blend to make. Force the collapse honestly: set the debt input the weighting formula reads to zero, with a rationale that states plainly this is a deliberate override — deposits and borrowings are funding the asset side of a spread business, not a financing claim on cash flows to equity — not a claim the issuer carries no debt. With that input at zero, the equity weight goes to one and the discount rate resolves to the cost of equity exactly. Disclose the override in the final report every time; do not let it sit as a silent input change.
- The free-cash-flow-to-equity chain produces equity value directly. Running the standard bridge on top of it — deducting debt, leases, preferred stock, adding back cash and non-operating investments — double-counts, because none of those items sit outside an equity cash-flow stream the way they sit outside an operating one. Zero every bridge line explicitly, each with a rationale stating it is already reflected in the equity cash flow, rather than leaving any of them to a default. Preferred dividends belong in the income statement above the free-cash-flow-to-equity line, not as a bridge deduction — build net income available to common net of them so the bridge preferred row is legitimately zero, not merely ignored.
- The terminal slot built for an EV/EBITDA exit does not fit a business with no EBITDA and no EV. Point it at the free-cash-flow terminal metric instead of the earnings-based one, and derive the multiple you apply to it from the price-to-tangible-book framework (see Terminal below) rather than from an EV/EBITDA comparable set — state in the rationale that the number is an equity multiple standing in for a slot built for an enterprise one.
- State the whole substitution up front, before writing a single formula: which cash-flow definition you built, that the discount rate is a forced cost-of-equity collapse and not the engine's native weighting, and that the terminal multiple is an equity multiple. A reader who only sees the numbers, not this paragraph, will otherwise read the chain as a mis-specified operating DCF rather than a deliberately reshaped equity one.
- Neither the historical nor the forecast chain needs a depreciation-and-amortization or capital-expenditure row built from operating logic; the modest premises-and-equipment spend a bank does carry is immaterial to its economics and does not deserve a driver assumption of its own. Leave it out of the cash-flow build rather than forcing a ratio-to-revenue story onto a number that was never the reinvestment story here.

## Revenue — the decomposition that pays

- Net interest income is average earning assets times net interest margin — build both factors as their own rows, never NII as one blended growth rate. Earning-asset growth is a balance-sheet story (loan and securities growth); margin is a rate-cycle story, and collapsing them into one NII growth number hides which one is doing the work.
- Decompose net interest margin into asset yield, funding cost, and deposit beta — the fraction of a policy-rate move that passes through to deposit pricing. A margin held flat while the rate path in your forecast moves is a claim that deposit beta and asset repricing offset exactly; state the mechanism or the flat margin is unearned.
- Split the loan book by category — commercial real estate, commercial and industrial, residential mortgage, credit card, auto — because each carries a different yield, a different loss profile, and a different point in the credit cycle. A single blended loan-growth and loss-rate assumption erases the fact that card losses run multiples of mortgage losses and CRE losses lag the cycle by years.
- Split non-interest income by type — deposit service charges, card interchange, wealth and asset-management fees, investment-banking and trading revenue, mortgage-banking fees. A higher and stickier fee share earns a higher multiple because it dampens the swings that pure spread income takes through a rate cycle; a trading-and-banking-heavy fee mix does the opposite and should not be priced like a wealth-management annuity.
- Wealth and asset-management fees scale with assets under management, so their forecast has two separate drivers — net client flows (a company-specific distribution and product story) and market appreciation (a macro assumption that must transmit through this named line, not sit as an unstated tailwind baked into a flat growth rate).
- For custody banks, invert the weighting: fee income (custody, fund administration, asset servicing, foreign exchange) is the primary line and net interest income is largely a function of client deposit balances and short-duration securities, not a loan book. Forecast their net interest income off client cash balances and the reinvestment rate on those balances, not off loan growth.
- The loan-category split also carries cycle timing, not just a different loss level: commercial real estate losses typically emerge years after origination as leases and appraisals reset, credit-card losses show up within a couple of quarters of unemployment turning, and residential mortgage losses trail even CRE in a normal cycle. A single downturn scenario applied uniformly across categories in the same forecast year misses that the categories do not take their losses together.
- Mortgage-banking income (origination and gain-on-sale) is a rate-sensitive, lumpy fee stream, not a steady annuity: it swells when rates fall and refinancing volume spikes, and compresses when rates rise and purchase volume alone remains. Forecast it off an origination-volume-and-margin mechanism tied to your own rate path, not as a flat share of revenue.

## Cost and margin — the level the story lives at

- The efficiency ratio — non-interest expense over the sum of net interest income and fee income — is this sector's cost story; forecast it directly rather than growing expense lines independently of the revenue base they support. A declining efficiency ratio needs a named driver: branch consolidation, technology spend replacing headcount, or fee income growing faster than the cost base that generates it.
- Provisioning is not a smooth cost. The expected-credit-loss reserving model puts a full-life loss estimate into the income statement the moment a loan is booked or the economic outlook darkens, so a reserve build ahead of a downturn depresses earnings before any loss is realized, and the release once losses have already been taken flatters earnings afterward. This is the sector's own form of the base-year trap: a base year sitting inside a reserve build or a reserve release is not a representative provision level, and anchoring next year's forecast on it inherits a number that a different point in the cycle would not produce.
- Separate net charge-offs (realized losses) from the provision expense (the income-statement charge that moves the reserve). The two converge only over a full cycle; a base year where provision runs well above or below net charge-offs is disclosing which way the reserve is moving, and that direction is itself the thing to forecast, not to ignore.
- Securities-portfolio income and mark-to-market or held-to-maturity treatment sit beside net interest income, not inside the loan-loss story; do not let a securities gain or loss driven by rate moves distort the read on core spread economics.
- Operating leverage — fee and net interest income growth outrunning expense growth — is the efficiency-ratio story's forward-looking twin: an efficiency ratio improving over the forecast should trace to a named revenue line growing faster than a named expense line, not to an expense growth assumption picked to hit a target ratio.

## Capital — retained earnings and the CET1 constraint

Reinvestment here is not physical. Asset growth consumes common equity: every dollar of loan and securities growth funded above the target common equity tier 1 ratio has to come from retained earnings (or a capital raise), so the equity a bank must retain to grow is the direct analogue of capex and working-capital investment in a normal chain, and it belongs in the free-cash-flow-to-equity build the same way capex belongs in free cash flow to the firm.

- Build the retention requirement explicitly: target CET1 ratio times the forecast growth in risk-weighted assets, translated into required equity growth, then free cash flow to equity is net income available to common less that requirement.
- Buybacks and dividends are the release valve, not a forecast input independent of capital generation: a bank generating capital faster than its asset growth consumes it returns the excess, and a payout ratio assumption disconnected from the CET1 math is asserting a capital position that either erodes or over-accumulates without limit.
- Regulatory stress tests set the required buffer above the regulatory minimum for the largest issuers; a target CET1 ratio meaningfully below the issuer's own stated operating buffer is a claim the buffer requirement eases, and that claim needs a named regulatory catalyst, not a trend line.
- A capital ratio running persistently above target with no acquisition pipeline and no announced buyback increase is not a free source of value sitting on the balance sheet — it is an assumption still owed a use, and leaving it un-deployed in the forecast understates the payout the capital math otherwise supports.
- A bank raising its CET1 target mid-cycle (post-stress, post-acquisition, ahead of a rule change) suppresses payout and free cash flow to equity for the transition years — model the glide path explicitly rather than jumping straight to the new steady-state ratio.
- Acquisition-funded growth consumes capital the same way organic risk-weighted-asset growth does, plus a goodwill and intangibles charge that permanently lowers tangible book value even when the deal is capital-accretive on a reported basis; treat a disclosed or expected deal as its own named event in the capital build, not folded into organic loan growth.
- Risk-weighted assets are not a fixed multiple of total assets: a mix shift toward higher-risk-weight categories (unsecured consumer, higher-leverage commercial real estate) consumes more capital per dollar of balance-sheet growth than a shift toward lower-risk-weight categories (agency-guaranteed mortgage, government securities), even at identical headline asset growth. Forecast the risk-weight mix alongside the loan mix, not as a constant ratio to total loans.

## Funding and the balance sheet — deposits, mix, and what "working capital" means here

There is no operating working-capital cycle to build; the analogous cash mechanics run through the deposit base and the securities portfolio instead.

- Funding mix — non-interest-bearing deposits, interest-bearing deposits, wholesale borrowings — sets both the level and the cyclicality of funding cost. A shift toward wholesale funding or interest-bearing deposits as a share of the total raises deposit beta and compresses margin even if headline deposit growth looks healthy; forecast the mix, not just the total.
- Deposit behavior (growth, and the beta of pricing to policy rates) is this sector's cash-timing story, playing the role working-capital days play elsewhere: deposit outflows into higher-yielding alternatives during a hiking cycle force replacement with costlier wholesale funding, and that substitution shows up in funding cost before it shows up in the deposit balance.
- The sign of this cycle runs opposite to a normal operating business's working-capital story: a bank's stress case is deposits leaving faster than assets can be shed, not receivables piling up — model deposit attrition and its funding replacement explicitly in any scenario that stresses the credit or rate assumptions, rather than assuming the funding side holds still while the asset side moves.
- Securities-portfolio marks matter for book value and capital even when accounted for as held-to-maturity and left out of earnings: an unrealized loss embedded in a held-to-maturity book is real economic exposure that would hit capital if realized, and a growth or capital-return story that ignores it is reading tangible book value as more available than it is.
- Tangible book value is a meaningful valuation anchor here in a way it is nowhere else in this series. Reconcile your forecast tangible book path against reported book value each period — a free-cash-flow-to-equity chain that implies tangible book growing faster than net income less payouts can explain has a formula error somewhere, not a sector quirk.
- Undrawn commercial lending commitments and letters of credit sit off the balance sheet but are a real claim on future funding the moment a borrower draws — a growth forecast for the funded loan book that ignores a large and growing unfunded commitment base is understating the funding and capital the business will need if utilization normalizes upward.

## Tax and WACC notes

- The effective tax rate sits close to the statutory federal-plus-state rate for most banks; municipal bond holdings and low-income-housing or renewable tax credits pull it down for issuers that lean into those books, and that gap is issuer-specific evidence, not a sector default to assume.
- Interest expense is a real, deductible cost of production for a bank, unlike the financing interest expense that a tax shield normally rewards in a levered non-financial issuer's discount-rate build; nothing about that changes how the effective tax rate itself is computed, only the framing — do not read a bank's tax position through the same debt-financing lens the discount-rate sheet was built around.
- The discount rate is the cost of equity, full stop — see Chain shape above for how to force the engine's weighted rate to collapse to it, and disclose the override every time you do it.
- Beta reflects operating leverage on a spread business plus whatever capital-markets or trading exposure sits alongside the lending book: a deposit-funded regional lender with little trading revenue sits low; a universal bank with a large markets and investment-banking arm sits meaningfully higher on the same balance-sheet leverage because its earnings volatility is higher. Do not apply one bank beta to an issuer whose revenue mix spans both.
- Custody banks carry lower beta than lending-heavy peers — fee revenue tied to assets under custody is more stable through a credit cycle than a loan book's provisioning swings, even though it is not immune to market-level swings in asset values.
- Cost of debt as a standalone number stops mattering once the discount rate collapses to the cost of equity; do not spend effort sourcing bond yields for this sector the way a levered non-financial issuer requires — the WACC sheet's cost-of-debt row becomes irrelevant to the number the model actually discounts at, once the debt weight is forced to zero.
- Hold the equity-risk-premium judgment at the same long-run figure you would use for any issuer; do not layer a separate financial-sector risk add-on on top of it. Beta already carries this sector's higher earnings sensitivity to the credit and rate cycle — stacking a sector premium on top of a beta that already reflects it double-counts the same risk.

## Terminal — what steady state means here

Terminal value here is a claim about a sustainable return on tangible common equity relative to the cost of equity, not a claim about a cash-flow growth rate standing alone. A bank earning its cost of equity in perpetuity is worth its tangible book value and nothing more — growth adds nothing when return equals the cost of capital that funds it. A bank earning above its cost of equity is worth a premium to tangible book, and the size of that premium is the multiple to defend. A bank earning below its cost of equity in perpetuity is worth a discount to tangible book, and that is a legitimate terminal case, not a modeling error to correct away — some franchises never earn their capital back and the valuation should say so.

- Use the identity: justified price-to-tangible-book equals (sustainable ROTCE minus terminal growth) divided by (cost of equity minus terminal growth). Build sustainable ROTCE, terminal growth and cost of equity as the three terminal judgments, and read the implied multiple off them rather than asserting a multiple directly.
- Feed that multiple into the free-cash-flow exit-value slot as an equity multiple applied to the terminal free-cash-flow-to-equity metric, not an EV/EBITDA multiple, and say so in the rationale — the slot's ordinary use does not fit this sector and the number needs to be legible as what it actually is.
- Check the perpetuity-growth path (free cash flow to equity, discounted at the cost of equity you forced the model to) against the price-to-tangible-book path for the same terminal year; a bank chain built correctly produces near-identical results from both, because they are the same claim expressed two ways. A material gap names a formula inconsistency between the earnings-based and the cash-flow-based build, not two legitimate scenarios.
- Terminal growth still cannot exceed long-run nominal GDP; a bank's loan book cannot outgrow the economy that borrows from it forever.

## Terminal-state calibration bands

These bands describe the steady state only — the economics the bank settles into once the current rate cycle and credit cycle are behind it, not the shape of the next two or three years. They must never set a near-term assumption: the explicit years are anchored on this issuer's own disclosed mix, its funding base and its own credit history, and a sector band sitting beside that evidence would compete with it and win for the wrong reasons. Terminal is the one place the issuer's own history cannot speak, because it is by construction the state after the cycle that history covers has ended.

Read the table as triage, not as a target — bands describe where issuers plausibly settle, and picking the midpoint because it is the midpoint is exactly the treatment the opening of this section warns against. A terminal input inside its band needs the ordinary rationale; one outside it is a claim that this issuer's steady state differs from its peers', and that claim needs evidence naming what makes the difference durable. The conventional reinvestment and working-capital rows from the generic chain do not apply at all here; they are replaced below by the capital and funding rows that actually bind.

## Failure modes specific to this sector

- **Running the generic free-cash-flow-to-the-firm chain unmodified.** Capex, D&A and operating-working-capital rows read as null or negligible for a bank, the engine computes a chain anyway, and the number that comes out is not free cash flow to equity — it is a number the model happily produces and that means nothing for this sector.
- **Leaving the weighted discount rate as computed.** A default weighting treats deposits and borrowings as the debt side of a capital structure, drives the equity weight far below one, and pulls the discount rate toward the cost of debt — understating the rate that should apply to an equity-only cash-flow stream by several points.
- **Double-counting through the standard equity bridge.** Deducting debt or lease liabilities from a value that free cash flow to equity has already arrived at net of financing understates equity value a second time on top of the first.
- **A base year sitting inside a reserve build or release.** Anchoring next year's provision on a base year where the reserve is actively building (post-downturn caution) or releasing (post-recovery normalization) inherits a number the cycle itself will reverse, not a representative run-rate.
- **A terminal ROTCE that ignores the capital it takes to sustain it.** A high sustainable-return assumption paired with a terminal CET1 ratio below the issuer's own operating buffer is asserting the bank can earn that return on less capital than its regulators and its own stated targets require.
- **Treating held-to-maturity securities marks as irrelevant because they bypass earnings.** They bypass the income statement, not the balance sheet; a capital-return or growth story that does not account for embedded securities losses is overstating the equity actually available.
- **Applying one beta or one fee-income share across a universal bank's whole franchise.** A bank with a large markets and investment-banking arm sitting beside a stable deposit franchise is two businesses with different earnings volatility; a single blended assumption for either understates the swings the trading side carries and overstates them for the deposit side.
- **Confusing custody-bank economics with a lending bank's.** Forecasting a custody bank's net interest income off loan growth, or its fee income off deposit-cycle dynamics, misreads a client-balance-and-servicing-fee business as a lending business it is not.
- **A capital-markets or trading-revenue line forecast on the spread-business growth rate.** Trading and investment-banking revenue is transactional and volume-driven, not a steady spread on a growing balance sheet; smoothing it into the same growth assumption as net interest income erases exactly the volatility that a universal bank's higher beta is pricing.
- **An implied price-to-tangible-book multiple never checked against where the sector actually trades.** A terminal identity built entirely from assumed ROTCE, growth and cost of equity can still produce a multiple far outside where any bank in the peer set has traded across a full cycle; treat that gap as a signal to revisit the three inputs, not as license to override the identity with a picked number.
- **A capex-and-D&A driver written into the chain out of habit.** The engine offers the row, premises-and-equipment spend is immaterial to a bank's economics, and a ratio-to-revenue assumption written for it anyway invents a reinvestment story the business does not have and clutters the chain with a driver that carries no informational content.
- **Loan growth forecast without its risk-weight consequence.** The same dollar of asset growth can consume very different amounts of capital depending on which category it lands in; a capital build that applies one blended risk-weight assumption across a loan mix shifting toward higher-risk categories understates how much retained equity that growth actually costs.
