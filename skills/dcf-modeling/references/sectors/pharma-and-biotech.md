# Sector playbook — Pharmaceuticals and Biotechnology

Covers large-cap diversified pharma (LLY, JNJ, PFE, MRK, ABBV, BMY), commercial-stage biotech with a concentrated marketed portfolio (AMGN, GILD, VRTX, REGN, BIIB), and clinical-stage biotech with little or no product revenue. The structural fact that breaks the generic chain: revenue here is a sum of product-level annuities with disclosed expiry dates. Every marketed drug carries a loss-of-exclusivity date fixed by patent and regulatory exclusivity law, and past it revenue does not decay gently — it falls off a cliff on a schedule you can read today. A single consolidated growth rate applied to this business is not a forecast; it averages a legally scheduled collapse against an unrelated launch curve and calls the blend a trend.

## Chain shape — what to build instead of the default

- Build revenue as a sum of named products, each with its own history, remaining exclusivity, and post-expiry shape — never a consolidated revenue.total growth assumption. Consolidated revenue is the sum, read after the products, not the driver.
- Decide which of three businesses you are modeling before writing anything: a diversified large-cap replacing cliffs continuously through pipeline and business development, a commercial-stage biotech whose value concentrates in a handful of franchises, or a clinical-stage issuer with no marketed product. The third case does not take this chain at all — see below.
- State the business-development posture explicitly at the top of the chain for any large-cap: does the forecast assume the issuer keeps buying its next decade of revenue at its historical pace, or does it run off the existing and disclosed pipeline alone. These are different claims about different cash uses and cannot both be silently true.
- Scale changes what the chain needs to hold together. A diversified large-cap's chain has to reconcile many products against one commercial and manufacturing cost base; a commercial-stage biotech's chain usually reduces to two or three products that carry the whole valuation, so get those few products right before touching anything else — a margin assumption error there moves the answer far more than the same error would at a diversified large-cap.
- Map each disclosed product or franchise to the revenue axis the spine mapping already promoted where one exists; where the filing's segment disclosure is coarser than the product list you need, build the product-level rows as your own custom breakdown and reconcile their sum back to the reported segment or consolidated total.

## Revenue — the decomposition that pays

### Marketed products — large-cap and commercial-stage

- For every material in-market drug, carry three pieces: current net sales, remaining exclusivity (patent expiry, regulatory exclusivity, any settled generic-entry date from litigation), and a post-expiry erosion path. Sum products to the franchise, franchises to total revenue.
- Erosion shape depends on modality, not on the issuer. Small-molecule drugs lose the large majority of unit volume within one to two years of generic entry because AB-rated generic substitution is automatic at the pharmacy. Biologics erode far more slowly against biosimilars — often over three to five years and rarely to zero — because substitution requires physician and payer action, interchangeability designation, and manufacturing scale-up the biosimilar maker must build. Apply the shape that matches the molecule, not a single cliff curve to the whole portfolio.
- For a drug inside its exclusivity window, forecast growth from volume (share, new indications, geographic expansion) and price separately — do not let a blended net-sales growth number hide a volume story funded by price or the reverse.
- For a disclosed near-term launch, build an uptake curve to peak sales and a subsequent decay, anchored on the issuer's own peak-sales guidance or disclosed trial population and pricing — not a generic launch curve. A launch curve with no named peak-sales anchor is an invented number wearing a formula.
- The exclusivity date itself is often not a single fixed number: a first-filer generic challenge, a patent-litigation settlement, or a pediatric-exclusivity extension can move it by months to years. Where the issuer discloses a settled date or a specific litigation outcome, use it over the nominal patent-expiry date on the label — the settled date is the harder evidence.

### Pipeline — risk-adjusted expected value

- Value the disclosed pipeline as a sum of program-level expected values: probability of technical and regulatory success by phase, times peak sales potential, times an uptake-and-decay curve once approved, discounted back from the expected approval date. Use the issuer's own disclosed pipeline, indications and trial readouts — a generic pipeline contribution manufactures the one number a DCF should never invent, because unlike a margin or a growth rate it has no company history to anchor it at all.
- Phase-transition probabilities rise sharply and non-linearly through development: they are lowest from first-in-human through proof of concept, roughly double moving into the pivotal registration-enabling phase, and are highest from filing to approval. Treat these as a calibration reference for triaging an issuer-specific estimate, never as the estimate itself — a named program's own trial design, endpoint and regulatory pathway (breakthrough designation, accelerated approval) shift its probability materially off the phase average.
- Do not let pipeline expected value silently double as both an operating-forecast input and a business-development justification; keep it in its own line so its risk-adjustment is visible.
- Distinguish internally discovered programs from in-licensed or acquired ones: an in-licensed program usually carries a royalty or milestone obligation back to the originator, which reduces the net economics the issuer actually captures relative to a fully owned program with the same peak-sales estimate. Read that obligation off the collaboration or license agreement, not off the headline peak-sales number.

### Clinical-stage biotech — no product revenue

- A conventional FCFF chain is the wrong tool here on its own: there is no revenue to decompose, no operating margin to hold flat or fade, and a forecast built on invented future product sales is a pipeline valuation wearing a DCF's clothes. Build the honest alternative instead — sum the risk-adjusted expected value of each disclosed program (same phase-probability, peak-sales, uptake-and-decay method as above) net of the company's ongoing operating cost to run itself, then check that figure against two things the pipeline sum ignores: cash runway to the next material readout, and the dilution a further capital raise at a plausible valuation would cost existing holders before that milestone.
- State the discount rate question honestly: a standard WACC assumes a going concern with a stable capital structure, and this issuer may have neither. If you discount program cash flows at a company cost of capital anyway, say so and say what it omits — financing risk and the probability of running out of cash before the next catalyst are not the same risk a beta-derived rate prices.
- Report this as what it is — a risk-adjusted pipeline value net of company cost, checked against runway and dilution — not as an enterprise DCF value with a caveat attached.

## Cost and margin — the level the story lives at

### Large-cap and commercial-stage

- Gross margin sits high across this sector because manufacturing cost is a small fraction of net price; the level moves with mix — royalty and collaboration revenue carries near-100% margin, biologics manufacturing costs more than small-molecule, and any product sold through a lower-margin channel (institutional, government) pulls the blend down. Forecast gross margin at the franchise level when franchises differ in mix, not as one consolidated ratio.
- R&D is this sector's reinvestment and it runs entirely through operating expense, never capex — do not read a flat or declining R&D ratio as margin discipline without asking whether it is instead the issuer eating its own future pipeline. A margin expansion plan that cuts R&D-to-revenue below the issuer's own multi-year floor is a claim that pipeline productivity does not need funding, and it needs a named cause.
- Selling and administrative cost includes the commercial infrastructure — sales force, market access, medical affairs — built ahead of a launch and sized to the product, not to current revenue; a pre-launch cost step-up shows up here before the revenue line moves, and it is evidence of the launch case, not overhead creep.

### Clinical-stage

- There is no gross margin to forecast because there is no product being sold; the entire cost base is R&D (trial conduct, manufacturing scale-up for trial supply) and the general and administrative cost of running a public company. Model these as absolute spend against the disclosed program timeline, not as a ratio to a revenue line that does not exist.
- Cost of goods for a pipeline this early is a scale-up and validation cost, not a per-unit manufacturing economic — do not build a gross-margin assumption off it merely because the line item exists on the income statement.
- Watch for a collaboration or milestone payment landing in revenue at a clinical-stage issuer: it is real cash but it is not product revenue, and treating it as the start of a growth trend forecasts a recurring stream out of what is usually a one-time or lumpy event tied to a specific trial milestone.

## Reinvestment — capex, D&A, and what maintenance means here

- Capex is manufacturing and lab capacity — physical plant — and is genuinely modest relative to revenue for most issuers in this sector, because the dominant reinvestment (R&D) already sits in operating expense. Do not treat a low capex-to-revenue ratio as underinvestment; it is normal here and does not need forecasting up to close a gap against D&A.
- D&A at any acquisitive large-cap is dominated by amortization of intangibles booked in business-development deals — acquired IPR&D, developed-product and marketing-authorization intangibles — which has no capex counterpart at all and fades toward zero as those deals age off the balance sheet, deal by deal, independent of any new capital spending. Split D&A into a PP&E-depreciation piece and an acquired-intangible-amortization piece before running any capex/D&A parity check; comparing capex against the blended D&A line will always show a permanent, unclosable gap that has nothing to do with underinvestment.
- Treat continued business development as its own funded line for any large-cap whose growth case relies on it, not as free upside layered onto organic reinvestment — it consumes cash (or debt or equity capacity) the same way capex does, and a forecast that assumes deal-driven revenue while funding no acquisition spend is inconsistent with itself.
- For a clinical-stage issuer, reinvestment is not a ratio to hold or fade at all — it is the disclosed trial budget for each program through its next milestone, and it stops or restarts on the same clock as the program itself rather than tracking any revenue base. Do not force this onto a capex-to-revenue or D&A-to-revenue driver; there is no revenue base for the ratio to mean anything against.

## Working capital — the cycle and its sign

- Inventory carries a genuinely long cycle here — manufacturing lead times plus mandatory quality release testing hold days-of-inventory well above most industrials — and it does not compress just because revenue growth accelerates; a forecast that shrinks inventory days while volume ramps for a launch is asserting a manufacturing efficiency gain that needs its own evidence.
- Rebate and chargeback accruals — the liability side of the gross-to-net bridge below — are frequently one of the largest current-liability balances on a pharma balance sheet and swing with both volume and the negotiated rebate rate; model them as their own component of operating working capital rather than folding them into a generic payables line, because a widening gross-to-net gap (below) inflates this accrual mechanically even when reported net revenue is flat.
- Net receivables from wholesalers and pharmacy benefit managers are collected against a price already net of most rebates, so do not double-count the gross-to-net adjustment in both the revenue line and the receivable days calculation.
- Wholesaler channel inventory can build ahead of a price increase or a disclosed loss-of-exclusivity date, the same buy-in dynamic seen elsewhere, followed by a destocking period once the reason for the buy-in passes; a revenue spike with no matching demand story often traces to this rather than to genuine volume growth.
- For a clinical-stage issuer, operating working capital is nearly irrelevant to the valuation and cash runway is the metric that actually governs survival — track cash, short-term investments and burn rate against the trial timeline instead of forecasting a working-capital ratio that has no real driver behind it.

## Tax and WACC notes

- Effective tax rates sit structurally below the US statutory rate because intellectual property — the patents and manufacturing rights behind the highest-margin products — is domiciled in low-tax jurisdictions. The OECD global minimum tax puts a floor under that structure for large multinationals; a terminal effective rate assumed to stay at the pre-minimum-tax historical level asserts the floor does not bind this issuer, and that needs a stated reason, not silence.
- Gross-to-net is a pricing mechanism, not a cost line, but it belongs in the WACC and terminal discussion because it is the channel through which US drug-pricing policy reaches this model: list price is not what the company collects. Rebates to pharmacy benefit managers, mandated government pricing (Medicaid best price, 340B), and — for products past the statutory eligibility window — negotiated prices under the US drug-price negotiation regime, all widen the gap between list and net price. A US revenue forecast that grows list price without growing the gross-to-net discount is not a volume forecast at all; it is a pricing assumption smuggled through the wrong line.
- Beta differs sharply between the two ends of this sector and must not be read off one "pharma beta": diversified large-cap issuers with broad marketed portfolios carry low beta, closer to a defensive consumer-staples profile, because portfolio breadth diversifies away single-product risk; commercial-stage and clinical-stage biotech carry materially higher beta, reflecting binary trial and regulatory outcomes concentrated in a small number of programs. Match the beta to where the issuer's revenue and pipeline concentration actually sit, not to its GICS label.
- A clinical-stage issuer typically has no meaningful pretax income to tax and carries accumulated net operating losses instead — an effective tax rate assumption is close to moot for the going-concern years, and a projected rate should reflect when the issuer plausibly turns profitable, not the sector's blended historical rate.
- Cost of debt for an investment-grade large-cap is straightforward to read off the issuer's own bond yields; a commercial-stage or clinical-stage biotech with no rated public debt has no such curve to read, and financing terms disclosed in a recent convertible or term-loan raise are the more honest source than a peer's rate.

## Terminal — what steady state means here

- A pharma or biotech at steady state is, by construction, still walking off the same cliff schedule that shapes the explicit years — patents keep expiring on a fixed clock. A positive terminal growth rate is therefore not a demographic tailwind riding through passively; it is an assertion that the issuer keeps replacing every future patent cliff through sustained research productivity, continued business development, or both, forever. Say explicitly which mechanism is doing that work in the terminal case, because "the population is aging" funds nothing on its own without a named pipeline or deal engine behind it.
- If the terminal case assumes continued business development funds part of steady-state growth, the terminal reinvestment intensity (below) must include that spend, not just PP&E capex and R&D — a terminal growth rate above what R&D-only reinvestment can plausibly sustain is quietly relying on acquisitions the reinvestment row never pays for.
- Terminal exit multiples should be read off comparables that share this issuer's terminal concentration profile: a diversified large-cap's terminal multiple belongs against other diversified large-caps, not against a concentrated commercial-stage peer whose value sits in one or two franchises, even if both wear the same GICS label today.
- A clinical-stage issuer does not get a perpetuity terminal value in the sense this section otherwise means: the risk-adjusted pipeline sum already prices each program to its own commercial horizon and decay, so a terminal growth rate layered on top of that sum double-counts durability the program-level valuation already assumed or denied. If a program's approved-product economics are themselves being carried into a perpetuity, treat that single product like any other marketed-product terminal case above, not like the company as a whole.

## Terminal-state calibration bands

The bands below describe steady state only — the economics after the explicit forecast, once the current product cycle, the disclosed patent-expiry wave and the named pipeline programs have all played out. They say nothing about next year and must not set a near-term assumption: the explicit years are anchored on this issuer's own marketed products, disclosed exclusivity dates and named pipeline, and a sector band placed beside that evidence would compete with it and win for the wrong reason. Terminal is the one place the issuer's own history cannot speak, because it is by construction the state after everything that history covers. Read the table as triage — inside the band needs the ordinary rationale, outside it needs evidence for why this issuer's steady state differs from its sector's — never as a target to hit.

Terminal capex and PP&E depreciation converge, the same as any sector, because steady state replaces the physical asset base rather than expanding it. Total D&A does not converge to capex here, because the amortization piece of D&A traces to acquired intangibles with no capex counterpart; split D&A before checking parity, and converge capex only against the PP&E-depreciation piece — the amortization piece should instead be fading toward whatever level continued, terminal-state business development implies, which is a judgment about deal pace, not a mechanical convergence. Terminal growth cannot exceed long-run nominal GDP growth.

| terminal driver | steady-state band | what puts an issuer at each end |
| --- | --- | --- |
| revenue growth | 2-4% | continuous cliff-replacement through pipeline and business development near the ceiling; an issuer relying on organic pipeline productivity alone with no deal engine nearer the floor |
| gross margin | 75-88% | a biologics-heavy, high-royalty mix near the ceiling; a portfolio with meaningful institutional or government-channel exposure and lower-margin legacy products near the floor |
| operating margin | 25-38% | scaled commercial infrastructure amortized over a broad portfolio near the ceiling; an issuer still carrying launch-stage commercial spend or R&D intensity above peers near the floor |
| R&D / revenue | 15-26% | this is the entry ticket to future cliff-replacement and does not shrink at maturity — a terminal margin funded by R&D below the issuer's own multi-year floor asserts pipeline productivity is no longer needed |
| selling and administrative / revenue | 20-30% | a concentrated, in-market portfolio with mature sales infrastructure near the floor; multiple simultaneous launches or a broad primary-care footprint near the ceiling |
| effective tax rate | 15-20% | the global minimum tax is the floor here exactly as elsewhere — a terminal rate below it asserts the IP-domiciling structure survives a regime built to stop it, and that needs a stated reason |
| reinvestment intensity (capex plus PP&E depreciation, as a share of revenue) | 4-7% | biologics manufacturing capacity and in-house fill-finish near the ceiling; an issuer that outsources manufacturing to contract manufacturers near the floor |
| operating NWC / revenue | 12-22% | long biologics manufacturing and QA cycles plus large rebate accruals push this positive and keep it there; a portfolio concentrated in low-rebate specialty or orphan products sits lower |
| beta (through-cycle, a WACC input rather than a terminal one) | 0.6-1.3 | a diversified, broad-portfolio large-cap at the bottom; a commercial-stage biotech with revenue concentrated in one or two franchises at the top |
| terminal growth | 2-3% | funded by a disclosed, sustained business-development pace and above-floor R&D productivity near the ceiling; organic pipeline productivity only, with no continued deal engine, nearer the floor — and never above nominal GDP regardless of mechanism |
| exit multiple — EV/EBITDA | 10-16x | a diversified large-cap with a broad, de-risked portfolio near the floor; a commercial-stage biotech with concentrated franchise growth and pipeline optionality near the ceiling |

## Failure modes specific to this sector

- **A consolidated revenue growth rate standing in for the product chain.** Blending a drug approaching loss of exclusivity with a drug mid-launch into one growth number produces a plausible-looking average that corresponds to no real product in the portfolio; the engine will compute it without complaint and the number will be wrong by construction.
- **An erosion curve applied to the wrong modality.** Using a small-molecule generic-cliff shape on a biologic overstates the post-exclusivity collapse; using a slow biosimilar-erosion shape on a small molecule understates it. The modality, not the sector label, sets the shape.
- **Pipeline value invented rather than sourced.** A generic phase-probability table applied to a made-up set of programs is not evidence — it is a number with the appearance of rigor. Use the issuer's own disclosed pipeline, named program by named program, or state plainly that the disclosure does not support a pipeline estimate at all.
- **Capex/D&A parity checked on the blended D&A line.** Because acquired-intangible amortization has no capex counterpart, this check will show large-cap pharma as perpetually under-investing in physical assets even when true PP&E reinvestment is entirely healthy. Split D&A first.
- **List-price growth mistaken for net-price growth.** Growing US revenue off list-price trend while holding the gross-to-net discount flat ignores the widening rebate and government-pricing gap, and ignores negotiated pricing for products that have crossed into the US drug-price negotiation regime's eligibility window.
- **A conventional DCF run on a clinical-stage issuer with no revenue.** Forecasting a margin-driven chain off zero or near-zero revenue produces formally valid but economically meaningless output; the honest tool is the risk-adjusted pipeline sum net of company cost, checked against runway and dilution, and the report should say that plainly rather than presenting a DCF number with a footnote.
- **Terminal growth treated as a demographic given.** An aging population supports pharma demand in the abstract, but it does not by itself replace this issuer's specific expiring patents — the terminal case still needs a named mechanism (R&D productivity, business development, or both) doing the replacement, funded in the terminal reinvestment row.
- **A perpetuity layered on top of a risk-adjusted pipeline sum.** The pipeline valuation for a clinical-stage issuer already carries each program to its own commercial decay; adding a further terminal growth rate on top values the same future cash flows twice under two different methods.
- **Business-development spend assumed but never funded.** A large-cap's terminal or explicit-year growth case that leans on continued deal-making needs to show the cash for it somewhere in the reinvestment row — a growth rate that requires acquisitions the model never pays for is not a coherent forecast, it is two forecasts that disagree with each other.
